# Chapter 2: Literature Survey (Existing System)

## 2.1 Overview of Existing System

The processing of examination results in engineering colleges has been carried out, for as long as the continuous assessment and end-semester examination system has existed, in a small number of recognisable forms. A survey of prevailing practice shows four approaches, each of which addresses part of the problem and none of which addresses the whole of it.

The first and oldest is the ledger approach. Results are recorded in ruled registers maintained by the examination cell, one book or one page per class and per subject, with totals and verdicts computed by hand. Marks arrive from the subject teacher on a printed or handwritten proforma sheet, are copied into the register, are totalled, are compared against the pass rules of the scheme in force, and are then transcribed into the class result register, from which the statement of marks is prepared. The approach requires no capital investment and leaves an auditable paper trail. Its weakness is equally well known: every figure in it is the product of a transcription, and the labour it demands scales with the size of the class and the number of subjects.

The second is the spreadsheet approach, which has largely displaced the register and is now the dominant working practice. Marks are captured in one worksheet per subject or per class, formulas are written to total the components and to test the pass conditions, and the class sheets are merged into a result sheet. The advantages over the register are immediate: totals are computed rather than written, formulas can be reused across a batch of classes, and a copy can be circulated without reproducing the original. The weaknesses are of four kinds. Correctness is limited by the diligence with which the formulas were written, since a copied range that should have been fixed produces a plausible but wrong answer in the very next row. Consistency is limited by the number of hands involved, since a class prepared in one semester by one person is rarely prepared in the next by the same person. Confidentiality is absent entirely, because the file is distributed by whatever means the office possesses and is protected by no enforceable rule. Accountability is likewise absent, because a spreadsheet records values but not authorship, so a change leaves no trace of who made it or on what grounds.

The third approach is the university result portal. Under this model the affiliating university provides the results-management infrastructure and the college acts as an uploading institution. Marks are uploaded in the format the university specifies, and the university performs the aggregation, applies the pass rules of the scheme it has defined, and publishes the result to students through the portal as a downloadable marksheet or a grade card. The significant advantage is that aggregation and publication are performed once, centrally and consistently for every affiliated college, which removes the aggregation error from the institution entirely. The limitations are of a different order. The institution's control is confined to the upload, because the calculation, the grading policy and the presentation of the result all belong to the university. The upload remains a manual transcription exercise, and because the format is fixed by the university rather than by the shape of the statement the college actually receives, the marks must first be re-keyed into the required layout, which reintroduces the very transcription risk the centralised calculation was intended to remove. The portal also provides no subject-level or class-level analysis: it publishes a result, not a distribution, and the analysis a department head needs remains a local manual exercise.

The fourth approach is the commercial college management system, the product category in which the full academic workflow is offered as licensed software. These systems are the most complete of the four and are widely deployed. They centralise marks entry, apply grading policies configured by the institution, produce student-facing grade cards and institutional analytics, and generally provide role-based access control with an audit trail. They are, however, shaped around a general-purpose institution rather than around a specific state technical university, and three gaps recur. Their data model holds the subject, its credits, the assessment components and the passing rule as administrative configuration rather than as a versioned curriculum rule set, so a college running three schemes simultaneously must configure three parallel instances of the machinery. Their marks-entry interfaces are designed for the college's own administrative staff entering marks that are already collated, not for the ingestion of printed statements, spreadsheets and scans, so the batch-capture problem remains outside the product. And their licensing is per-seat and per-module, which places a recurring commercial cost against a requirement the institution must meet at the end of every examination cycle.

Against this background the present system occupies a distinct position. It does not attempt to replace the university result portal, which remains the authority for the formal declaration of a result, nor does it attempt to replicate the breadth of a commercial college management system. It is directed at the segment of the workflow where the risk actually lies: the period between the arrival of the raw marks in the examination cell and the point at which a defensible, analysable and correctly governed record of those marks exists. It differs from the spreadsheet approach in that the aggregation is performed by scheme-aware calculation rather than by formulas written by hand, and in that a missing mark cannot be mistaken for a zero. It differs from the portal in that it treats the printed statement, the spreadsheet and the scan as first-class inputs rather than requiring the college to re-key them into a prescribed layout. It differs from the commercial systems in that it is built around a versioned curriculum rule set and the simultaneous operation of three schemes, in that it makes the ingestion of batch marks a primary capability, and in that it can be run without a per-seat licence charge.

## 2.2 Limitations of Existing System

The limitations identified in the survey above are stated individually, since each motivated a specific requirement in the system that is described in Chapter 7.

1. **Error-prone manual aggregation.** The totals, percentages, credits and verdicts of the manual and spreadsheet approaches are the product of transcription and of formulas written and rewritten by successive users. A copied range that should have been fixed produces a correct-looking answer in one row and a wrong answer in the next; a mistyped roll number attaches a mark to the wrong student. Because these errors propagate into every derived figure, the characteristic pattern is not a visibly broken total but a plausible one, which is why they survive the review that normally accompanies result preparation.

2. **Delayed result declaration.** The manual cycle for a hundred-plus-student class runs to two to three days. The delay is not caused by the absence of effort but by the sequential nature of the work: marks must be copied, totalled, checked against the pass rules, merged across subjects and reformatted into the statement, and each stage waits on the previous one.

3. **No per-subject difficulty analysis.** While an overall pass rate may be available, the standing of an individual subject is not. Nothing distinguishes a subject in which a small minority struggled from one in which the entire cohort struggled, and the subject teacher's request for such an indication cannot be answered without repeating the entire manual exercise over the historical mark sets.

4. **Weak access control in shared spreadsheets.** The spreadsheet has no capacity to distinguish a reader from a writer. A subject teacher asked to enter marks for three subjects can open the marks of every other subject in the college; a supervisory member of staff can alter the marks of a subject they do not teach. The available control is procedural, and procedural control without enforcement is advisory.

5. **Poor audit trail.** Neither the register nor the spreadsheet records authorship. A mark altered in good faith after a query leaves no trace of the original value, of the person who made the change, of the time, or of the reason given. The institution's ability to answer a queried result is therefore limited to the recollection of the individual who happened to make the entry.

6. **No bulk import from printed statements.** No existing facility takes a printed result statement as it arrives and turns it into structured mark entries. The institution's input is a wide table with merged headers, subject blocks separated by delimiters, summary columns duplicating figures the system would rather compute, absent candidates, candidates whose result was withheld, and components that were not conducted and therefore appear blank. The marks must therefore be re-keyed by hand, and every re-keying is an opportunity for the errors described in the first limitation.

7. **Inconsistent application of scheme rules across departments.** Three curriculum schemes operate simultaneously, each with its own component decomposition and thresholds, and the person doing the aggregation is rarely equally familiar with all three. Where the wrong threshold set is applied, the totals are arithmetically correct and the verdicts look reasonable, but a student who should have passed is shown as having failed a component. Because the error lies in the rule applied rather than in the arithmetic, re-checking the arithmetic cannot detect it, and the discrepancy frequently surfaces only after publication.

8. **Scalability limits.** The manual and spreadsheet approaches scale with the square of effort as volume grows, because every additional subject multiplies the number of subject-wise totals and every additional year multiplies the history a departmental meeting might want to consult. The point at which the process becomes untenable arrives without warning.

9. **Difficulty in extracting per-student multi-year trends.** A student's progression across semesters is the natural unit of academic counselling, but the marks of successive semesters live in successive files, under successive schemes, in formats that change between years. Assembling a continuous academic history for one student is a manual exercise in its own right, and it is performed rarely.

10. **Absence and non-conduction cannot be distinguished.** In the manual process, an absent candidate, a component that was not conducted and a genuine zero are all recorded as a zero, and the difference between them is lost at the moment of entry. This is the origin of the most damaging single behaviour of the manual process, namely the treatment of a not-yet-entered mark as a zero, which reports a half-entered class as a class in which every student has failed.

11. **Inconsistent reporting.** Statements, mark sheets and analytics are prepared afresh each cycle from the same underlying marks, and they differ between cycles and between preparers. The figures quoted to a student, in a departmental meeting and in a board report are not guaranteed to agree, and there is no means of establishing which is correct.

12. **Manual topper lists mishandle ties.** Where two or more students share the highest mark in a subject or a class, the manual list typically records one of them, and the others are omitted from a distinction they have earned. Where a candidate's result was withheld or the candidate was absent, the list may also count a mark that should have been excluded, and both conditions are corrected only after an objection.

## 2.3 Proposed System

The proposed system is a web application that performs the entire result-analysis workflow of a single engineering college, from the configuration of its academic structure through the ingestion and entry of marks, the computation of results under the correct scheme, the analysis of performance, the publication of statements and the supervision of every change made along the way. It is built around a relational database holding 23 entities, an access model of 45 permissions over 20 resources assigned to six roles, a computation layer containing one calculation strategy for each of the three curriculum schemes, and a background processing layer that handles document extraction and report generation. Its distinguishing characteristic is that no academic figure is transcribed: totals, percentages, credits, verdicts and rankings are derived from the mark entries, and the summary columns that the source statements themselves print are re-derived rather than trusted.

Marks reach the system by two routes. In the first, the teacher enters them directly, component by component or grid-wise for a whole class, with absence and withheld results recorded as distinguishable states. In the second, a file is uploaded and is parsed into rows. The parser is written for the printed statement as the examination cell actually receives it, and it handles the conditions a generic table reader would get wrong: a subject heading is recognised by its shape so that a column label is never mistaken for one, the college's own summary columns are ignored and re-derived, the practical block is kept separate from the theory block of the same subject, a blank cell is reported as a component that was not conducted rather than written as a zero, and an absent candidate is written as zero with an absence flag while a failure is written as zero without one. Nothing reaches the mark record without a person confirming it, and the confirmation is performed by someone whose own rights determine how much of the file they may write: the examination cell confirms the whole class, while a teacher confirms only the subjects allocated to them, with unallocated rows skipped and listed rather than causing the entire upload to fail.

The curriculum itself is read from the scheme document. A deterministic stage locates the course table and reduces it to a positional slice, because the document family is unbounded and a purely rule-based reading fails silently with plausible-but-wrong figures; a language model then interprets that slice into structured course rows, and a separate screening stage inspects the document for instructions aimed at the model before the model reads it, with the verdict shown to the reviewer. Every proposed subject appears in an editable review table, with dropped rows shown rather than hidden. Where the reading service is unavailable, the same review table is filled in by hand.

Results are computed by three separately implemented strategies, selected automatically from the scheme recorded against each student, and each returning full precision with rounding applied only at presentation. The rule that unrecorded marks are pending rather than failed is applied consistently across the computation, the reports, the analytics and the dashboard. A student's result is complete only when every offered subject carries a verdict, and a report is refused for a class in which any result is incomplete, with the refusal naming the subjects outstanding and the students affected. The download of a report regenerates the file from the current marks rather than serving a stored copy, so that the readiness check is performed again at the moment of download.

Table 2.1: Comparison of existing and proposed approaches

| Aspect | Existing Approach | Proposed System |
|---|---|---|
| Marks capture | Marks re-keyed by hand into registers or spreadsheets; each transcription risks error | Statements, spreadsheets, comma-separated files, text documents and scans parsed into rows; blanks read as non-conducted components |
| Evaluation rules | Rules of one scheme applied by the person aggregating, with no enforcement of the correct scheme | Three strategies, one per scheme, selected automatically from the student's record and independently testable |
| Scheme handling | Three schemes managed informally; wrong thresholds may be applied while the arithmetic stays correct | Scheme rules held as versioned configuration; subject list read from the curriculum document under human review |
| Access control | A shared file with no distinction between reader and writer; control is advisory | Permission-based authorisation with 45 permissions over 20 resources, scope applied in the business logic |
| Audit trail | No record of authorship; an alteration leaves no trace of the original value, person or reason | Append-only trail of every academic change recording actor, action, resource, reason and request context |
| Analysis | Overall pass rates at best; subject difficulty and multi-year trends are manual exercises | Class- and subject-level statistics, difficulty labels, trends across years, toppers with tie detection and three ranking rules |

The proposed system addresses the limitations set out in Section 2.2 directly. The error-proneness of manual aggregation is removed by computing every derived figure from the mark entries and by re-deriving the summary columns of the source statements in place of trusting them; the delay in declaration and in analysis is removed by ingesting a whole class in one operation. The absence of subject-level difficulty analysis is addressed by statistics computed per student and then banded, together with a difficulty label derived from each subject's pass rate. The weakness of access control in shared files is addressed by a permission model in which scope is derived from the caller and never accepted as a request parameter. The absence of an audit trail is addressed by a trail that records every academic mutation, with corrections and privilege changes under distinct actions of their own. The inconsistent application of scheme rules is addressed by the automatic selection of the calculation strategy from the student's own scheme, so that no human judgement is involved in the choice. The scalability limit is addressed by a database whose access paths are indexed for the recurring queries and whose integrity is enforced by constraints, and the difficulty of assembling per-student multi-year trends is addressed by recording marks against an academic year and a student, with the history therefore already separated.

## 2.4 Feasibility Study

### Technical Feasibility

The system is technically feasible because every element it depends upon is a current, mainstream and widely supported technology, and because the design deliberately avoids depending on anything exotic. The presentation layer is a modern component-based web framework rendering a responsive interface that adapts to mobile, tablet and desktop viewports; the application layer is a structured server framework whose discipline of separating the request handler, the business logic and the data access into distinct layers is directly reflected in the module structure of the system; the data layer is a relational database reached through a typed object-relational mapper that generates its client from a single schema definition, so that the database and the application cannot drift apart. Every one of these is in wide institutional use and supported beyond the lifetime of this project. Type checking is enforced throughout in strict mode, and the language target is a currently supported standard, so the code is portable across the mainstream operating systems on which a college's staff are likely to work.

The external infrastructure has been chosen so that the institution is not obliged to purchase or maintain a server. The database is a managed, serverless relational service, and the implementation is written with its behaviour in mind: the connection pool is capped because the service limits concurrent connections, transient connectivity failures are retried because the service suspends after inactivity, and the establishment of the initial connection is itself retried so that the application starts against a cold service rather than failing. Object storage is addressed through a standard interface that works with the managed offering as well as with the widely used alternatives, and it falls back to local disk when no bucket has been configured. Caching and background job processing are provided by an in-memory data store available as a hosted service and equally as a local instance. In every case the institution's requirement is a configuration value rather than a capital purchase.

The two capabilities that depend on artificial intelligence or optical character recognition are the ones that would usually threaten technical feasibility, and both have been designed so that they cannot become dependencies. The document recognition capability used for scanned result statements selects among three recognition models according to a content hint and feeds their output through the same parser that handles an ordinary spreadsheet, so that the review screen has one shape regardless of the source. The curriculum-reading capability reduces a document to a table that a deterministic stage has selected, has that table interpreted under a strict output schema at zero sampling temperature, has the document screened for instructions aimed at the model before the model reads it, and validates every proposed row against the component rules afterwards. When either service is unreachable, the corresponding screen continues to function: the upload area is replaced by a message offering manual entry, and the same review table is filled in by hand. No part of the result workflow therefore depends on the availability of a paid third-party service or on the correctness of an unreviewed machine reading, since no machine reading is ever written to the record without a person confirming it.

The remaining technical considerations are conventional and have been addressed within the implementation. The mark table is expected to hold hundreds of thousands of rows against a student population in the thousands, so the recurring queries have been indexed, the principal computations are performed against the class context rather than the whole catalogue, and the aggregate statistics are computed per student rather than per mark row. Upload sizes are capped at the levels at which the format is genuinely used, with a fifty megabyte limit on result files and a five megabyte limit on student imports. Requests are protected against denial of service by three tiers of rate limiting with route-specific tightening on the operations that are expensive or abusable, such as sign-in, file upload and report generation. Long-running work is executed on background queues with pollable status rather than held open in a request, and each job falls back to inline execution if the queue is unavailable, so that no upload or report is lost. The cache is treated as an accelerator and never as an authority: if it is unreachable, reads fall through to the database and writes are discarded, and the system continues to return correct results.

### Economic Feasibility

The economic case rests on two observations. The first is that the system is built entirely from components available under licences permitting use without a per-seat charge. The database, the cache, the server framework, the web framework, the object-storage client, the charting library, the spreadsheet-generation library and the language runtime are all obtainable at no licence cost, and the user interface components are built on openly published primitives rather than on a proprietary suite sold per seat. Consequently the marginal cost of admitting the ninety-nine additional members of the college beyond the first is zero in licence terms, which is the opposite of the position in the commercial software approach, where a licence charge recurs for every additional member of staff and every additional module enabled.

The second observation concerns what the system does and does not require in the way of infrastructure. No server is purchased, no on-premises database instance is installed, and no capacity planning is required for local hardware, because the database, the object storage, the cache and the queue all exist as managed services subscribed for by capacity rather than owned as assets. The recurring expenditure therefore consists of the subscriptions for the managed database, object storage and cache, and the hosting of the mail service used for the two transactional messages the system sends, the invitation and the password reset. Against a college of five branches and an expected student population in the low thousands, these are modest and predictable monthly costs with no licence component and no annual escalation clause.

The investment that the project principally demands is time rather than capital. The analytical work of the three curriculum schemes, the reading of the printed statement format, the modelling of the access rules and the verification of the whole workflow are intellectual effort that the institution has to supply in any case, because the same understanding is required to run the manual process correctly; the system converts that existing investment into a repeatable capability rather than requiring a new one. The benefit is realised in two measurable ways. The first is the compression of the result-processing cycle from two to three days to minutes, which releases examination-cell effort for the parts of the job that require judgement and for the teaching itself. The second is the removal of a class of error that cannot be detected by checking arithmetic: because the correct scheme is selected automatically and because a missing mark cannot be recorded as a zero, the two most expensive categories of error in the manual process are eliminated rather than merely reduced. A third benefit is not directly financial but is properly part of the economic case, since it reduces institutional risk: a defensible audit trail and a controlled approval step mean that a queried result can be answered from a record rather than from recollection.

Against these benefits the cost profile is modest and front-loaded only in time, and the system does not create a new recurring obligation of the kind that the licensed alternatives impose. No dependency on a paid third-party service exists, since the two recognition capabilities degrade to manual entry when unavailable and therefore cannot generate an unbudgeted operational cost at the point of use. The economic feasibility of the project is therefore sound, and the principal risk to it is not financial but one of adoption, which is a matter of training and of the discipline of confirming uploaded marks rather than a matter of budget.

### Legal Feasibility

The system processes the academic records of one institution, and the institution is therefore the party entitled to hold and determine those records. The data concerned is academic in character: the branch, semester, scheme, academic year, batch, roll number and name of each student, the subjects offered, the marks obtained in each component, the resulting credits, grades and verdicts, and the assignment of teaching and class-teacher responsibilities to members of staff. The personal data within that is deliberately narrow — a name, an email address, a roll number, and optional contact, category and guardian details — and the system holds it under the control of the institution's own administrators, with no access outside it except where the institution has itself configured an external service.

That narrowing of personal data is a deliberate design position, and it is enforced in the mechanism rather than merely stated. Access is governed by a permission model in which the ordinary student role carries four rights, covering the dashboard shell, the student's own record, the student's own marks and the student's own class leaderboard; the general right to read the student list is deliberately withheld, so the permission set cannot expose the roster or the marks of others even if it were granted in error. The student is resolved from the authenticated session rather than from anything the client supplies, so a request for another student's record cannot be constructed even by a hand-crafted request, and a request for another student's upload is answered as though it did not exist rather than as forbidden, so that the existence of an identifier cannot be probed. The same discipline applies throughout: a member whose scope does not include a requested branch is refused rather than silently redirected, and the refusal is uniform so that it cannot be used as an oracle.

Documents uploaded to the system — printed result statements, scanned pages, curriculum documents and profile pictures — are held for the institution's own purposes and are not redistributed. No uploaded document is published, shared or transmitted outside the institution's configured storage and its authenticated interface. No third party acquires any ownership of uploaded content: the recognition services receive a document only in order to perform the extraction requested, are not granted a licence over it, retain nothing beyond the operation itself, and are never given the right to decide anything about an individual. Their use is confined to returning recognised characters, which are then parsed by the institution's own logic and reviewed by a person before anything is written. Where the recognition service is not configured, no document leaves the institution at all, because the manual path processes it in place.

Passwords are stored as salted bcrypt hashes computed at twelve rounds, and the hash never leaves the data layer; the credential presented at sign-in is verified against the stored hash and discarded. Refresh, password-reset and invitation tokens are stored only in hashed form, so that the plaintext token exists solely in the link delivered to the intended recipient, and each such link has a defined lifetime — thirty minutes for a password reset and seven days for an invitation — after which it is of no effect, with only the most recent reset link remaining valid. The invitation mechanism sets no temporary password at all; the invitee chooses their own credential when accepting, which removes the practice of communicating a credential by other means. The password-reset procedure returns an identical response whether or not the address is registered, and all invalid invitation tokens produce one indistinguishable message, so that neither procedure can be used to establish whether a particular person holds an account. Access tokens are short-lived and are refreshed by rotation, and the session is revoked on sign-out, on sign-out-everywhere and on the removal of a member.

The audit trail supports the accountability on which the institution's defensibility rests, recording for every academic change the actor, the action, the resource and its identifier, the stated reason where the operation warrants one, and the context of the request. Corrections and privilege changes are recorded under actions of their own rather than as ordinary updates, because they overwork or enlarge the authority that another person is responsible for, and because distinguishing them is what allows the trail to be read as an accountability record rather than as a log of clicks. The trail is append-only, is written only for changes that actually took effect, and is exposed to those with a supervisory function subject to the same scope rules that govern everything else. Within the limits stated in the system, the legal and regulatory position of the project is therefore sound: the institution remains the holder of its academic records, personal data is minimal and access-controlled, third-party services are used only as processors of a submitted document and never as decision-makers about an individual, credentials are handled to standard practice, and the recorded trail makes the institution able to account for every change it has made.
