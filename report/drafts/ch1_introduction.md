# Chapter 1: Introduction & Problem Definition

## 1.1 Problem Statement

An engineering college is required, at the end of every examination cycle, to convert raw marks into a result that is arithmetically correct, procedurally defensible and communicable to every student. In the institution this system serves, that task is performed for five engineering branches, each running eight semesters, each semester carrying six to eight subjects, and each subject assessed through continuous assessment, an internal paper, an end-semester written paper and, in some cases, a practical. A class of a hundred-plus students across six or seven subjects produces several hundred individual mark entries before any aggregation takes place, and when this work is performed by hand the examination cell consumes two to three working days before a result can be declared.

The academic structure itself complicates the task. Three curriculum schemes run at the same time — the 2019, the 2022 and the 2024 revisions — and they are not merely renumbered editions of one another. The 2019 scheme assesses a subject through two continuous assessments, a mid-semester paper, a written theory paper and a practical, and requires every component to clear its own threshold as well as the aggregate: each continuous assessment at least 6 of 20, the mid-semester at least 8 of 20, the internal total at least 30 of 60, the written paper at least 45 of 100, the practical at least 10 of 25, and the aggregate at least 50 per cent. The 2022 and 2024 schemes instead split the theory paper of 100 into an internal assessment of 40 and an external assessment of 60, with the practical of 25 separate, and require an internal total of 20 of 40, an external total of 30 of 60, a practical of 13 of 25 and the same aggregate. A result computed correctly under one scheme is frequently wrong under another, and a cell applying the rules of the scheme it knows best to a class belonging to another produces a wrong answer with no indication that it has done so.

The inputs themselves are unwieldy. The marks do not arrive as a clean table. They arrive as a printed result statement in which one row per student is laid out across a wide sheet of merged two-row headers and subject blocks separated by delimiters; as spreadsheets whose header wording varies between offices; as text-layer documents; and as scans of printed pages containing no extractable text at all. The human reading of such a statement is the point at which most errors enter the process. A subject code that resembles a column label, a practical block printed in the same columns as the theory block of the same subject, a blank cell denoting a component that was not conducted rather than one scored zero, a student marked absent as distinct from a student who failed, an over-maximum mark produced by a typographical slip in the source — each is a condition a manual reading can get wrong, and each distorts every figure computed downstream from it. Most seriously of all, a missing mark is commonly recorded as a zero, so that a class whose marks for one subject have not yet been entered is reported as one in which every student has failed that subject, and the error propagates into the totals, the grades, the topper list and the published statement without visible symptom.

The consequences of these errors are not confined to the arithmetic. Once declared, a result becomes the basis on which progression, eligibility for further examinations and, in some cases, admission and scholarship decisions rest, so a single mis-tallied subject or an incorrectly awarded credit is a defect with consequences beyond the marksheet on which it appears. Manual practice also handles ties poorly: where two students share the highest mark, the list typically names one, and the other is left without the distinction earned.

Alongside the computational problem sits an administrative one. Marks are held in shared spreadsheets and text files passed between the teacher, the head of the department, the examination cell and the principal. Such a file has no notion of who may open it: the teacher asked to enter marks for three subjects can also open the marks of every other subject in the college, and the principal, who needs only to read, can also alter. There is likewise no record of what was changed, by whom, when or why, so a correction made in good faith overwrites the original without trace. Reporting compounds the problem: the statements, mark sheets and analytics the institution must produce are assembled each cycle from the same manual sources and differently each time. The figures quoted in a board report, in a departmental meeting and to a student are not guaranteed to agree, and the standing of an individual subject cannot be established without a fresh and equally laborious manual exercise.

It is against this background that the present system was conceived and built. It takes raw marks in the forms in which they actually arrive, converts them into a single normalised record of mark entries, and from that record computes a result that is arithmetically derived rather than transcribed, under rules selected automatically from the scheme to which each student belongs. It records who did what, when and why, and restricts who may see and who may change each part of the data according to their role. The remainder of this chapter states the objectives that guided that construction, the boundaries within which it was built, and the methodology through which it was produced.

## 1.2 Objectives

Each objective below is expressed as a concrete, verifiable outcome against which the finished system is assessed in Chapter 7.

1. To model the academic structure of an engineering college faithfully — branches, schemes, academic years, batches, semesters, subjects, offerings, examinations, class-teacher assignments, allocations and students — in a single normalised database of 23 entities with referential integrity enforced by the database rather than by convention.

2. To implement the computation rules of the three curriculum schemes as three separately implemented, independently testable calculation strategies selected automatically from the scheme on each student's record, so that no class is ever evaluated under another scheme's rules.

3. To establish the treatment of an unrecorded mark as pending rather than failed, so that a subject with no marks carries no verdict, yields no credits and is counted separately from a failure, and a result is complete only when every offered subject has a verdict.

4. To permit marks entry in the form assessment actually takes: component by component for a single student and grid-wise for a whole class, with absence and withheld results recorded as distinguishable states rather than zeroes, and saving restricted to the cells the user touched.

5. To ingest marks in the formats in which they reach the examination cell — printed result statements, spreadsheets, comma-separated files, text documents, text-layer documents and scans — through a parser that recognises the awkward conditions of the source format, including a blank cell as a non-conducted component and an absent candidate as distinct from a failure.

6. To require human confirmation before any extracted mark reaches the record, with the confirmer's reach determined by their own rights, so that a teacher confirms only their allocated subjects while the examination cell confirms the whole class, and unallocated rows are skipped and listed rather than failing the upload.

7. To derive a scheme's subject list from the curriculum document itself, with a deterministic stage selecting the table and a language model interpreting it, a screening stage inspecting the document for instructions aimed at the model beforehand, and a human examiner correcting every proposed subject before it is saved.

8. To enforce access control on permissions rather than role names, with 45 permissions over 20 resources assigned to six roles, and with branch scoping and allocation-based ownership applied in the business logic so that a member sees only the branches and subjects they are responsible for.

9. To maintain an append-only audit trail of every academic change recording the actor, the action, the resource and its identifier, a stated reason where the operation warrants one, and the request context, exposed in a form filterable by action, resource and member.

10. To deliver analysis and reporting derived from the same records rather than transcribed from them, comprising college-, branch-, class- and subject-level statistics with trends across academic years, topper lists with explicit tie detection, and seven report types exported as spreadsheets, with generation refused while any result in the class remains incomplete.

## 1.3 Scope of the Project

The system is built for the complete result-analysis workflow of a single engineering college. Its scope extends from the configuration of the academic structure through the entry and ingestion of marks, the computation of results, the analysis of performance, the publication of statements and the supervision of every change made along the way. A single college is served, and the branch is the partitioning unit within it; the isolation of one institution from another is not a goal and has not been built.

Within that boundary the scope is wide. The academic structure is fully configurable: branches, academic years, curriculum schemes with their rule documents, subject identities with their assessment component breakdowns, and the placement of subjects into class contexts are maintained through the system rather than assumed. Student records are created individually or imported in bulk from a spreadsheet, with the branch, scheme, academic year, batch and semester applied once to the imported set and any conflicting class column in the sheet ignored rather than permitted to override. Marks are entered manually at component level or in bulk grids, or ingested from uploaded files, and are always subject to a scheme-appropriate computation. Six roles are supported, distinguished by 45 permissions: the examination cell operates the academic data, the teacher only allocated subjects, the head of the department one branch, the principal supervises without any write right, the student only their own record, and the super administrator configures the institution while remaining read-only on academic data.

Several capabilities are deliberately outside the scope of the project, and their absence is a considered decision rather than an omission. There is no re-evaluation workflow: a withheld flag and an audited correction route exist, so that a result can be marked as withheld or a teacher's entry formally overwritten with a written reason, but there is no representation of a student applying for re-evaluation, no re-evaluator role, and no provisional and final result states. There is no attendance model, since attendance does not participate in any pass or fail determination, and no best-of rule and no grace marks.

The report output of the system is a spreadsheet and only a spreadsheet. Seven report types are produced, and the complete report bundles seven sheets, but there is no document-format report generator, no print-specific layout and no other export format; the document-reading components present in the system exist to read curriculum and result documents, not to produce them. There is no mobile application: the interface is a responsive web application that adapts to mobile, tablet and desktop viewports, but no separate application is provided. There is no multi-institution tenancy, no parent portal, and no fee, admission, placement or library module.

Two further boundaries concern how the system behaves rather than what it contains. The external recognition services used for reading scanned documents and curriculum documents are accelerators and not dependencies: when either is unavailable the affected screen offers manual entry in place of automated reading, so that no part of the result workflow is blocked by the absence of a paid service. And the deployed hosting configuration is inferred from the structure of the implementation rather than asserted as an artefact that exists.

## 1.4 Methodology Overview

The system was developed by a phased methodology in which each phase produced a defined output that the following phase consumed, and in which verification was performed alongside construction rather than deferred to the end. The overall workflow of the system is illustrated in Figure 1.1, and the development methodology followed during the construction of the system is illustrated in Figure 1.2.

The first phase was a study and analysis of the requirements. The examination domain was examined as it exists rather than as it was assumed to exist, and the outcome was a description of the academic structure, the three scheme rule sets, the roles and the responsibilities of the examination cell. Two consequential observations were recorded: that the three schemes differ structurally, not only in threshold values, which required the calculation logic to be expressed as distinct strategies rather than as one parameterised rule; and that an absent mark is a distinct condition from a zero mark, whose treatment as a zero would misreport partially entered classes. The second observation became the defining rule of the computation layer.

The second phase was data and access modelling. The entities of the academic domain were identified, their relationships established and their cardinalities fixed, and the access model was deliberately separated from the data model. Authorisation was expressed as permissions rather than as role names, because a role is a label applied to a person while the operations a person may perform are the things that require control. Six roles were defined as named bundles of 45 permissions over 20 resources, with branch scoping and allocation-based ownership as the two dimensions along which a permission narrows further. A set of invariants was derived at this stage and carried into the third phase, that of database and interface design, where they were expressed as unique constraints rather than application checks: one offering of a subject per class context, at most one class teacher per class context, one allocation per teacher, subject and year, roll numbers unique within an academic year rather than globally, and an idempotency key over the student, offering, component and year that makes re-importing the same data harmless. The indexes required by the recurring queries were created at the outset, and the interface was designed around the resolution of scope before any data was fetched, so that the server determines what the member is entitled to see and the screens render that scope.

The fourth phase was incremental implementation, in which each functional area — identity and access, academic configuration, students, marks and evaluation, file upload and extraction, scheme reading, result viewing, analytics, reports, toppers, class overview, dashboard, audit and notifications — was built, exercised and reviewed before the next was begun. The fifth phase was testing conducted alongside implementation rather than after it: automated suites were written for the calculation strategies, the statement parser, the permission catalogue and its agreement with the seeded role definitions, the scope and address-guard services, the document guard and the scheme reader, and a manual browser-driven verification was carried out across seven role contexts.

The sixth phase was verification against real data, in which the system was exercised with a realistic printed result statement and with realistic mark sets drawn from the seeded academic structure, and the figures produced were compared with hand-derived figures. The seventh and final phase was hardening and documentation, in which the limits of the implementation were probed and recorded rather than concealed: the absence of virus scanning on uploads, the absence of a cross-site request forgery mechanism, the fallback behaviour of the token secrets when their environment configuration is absent, the divergence between the pass threshold applied in computation and in analytics, and the mixture of client-side and server-side filtering on some list screens were each identified and left visible. A principle governed the treatment of evidence throughout: a claim was recorded as verified only when it had been observed, by running the code, by exercising the interface or by comparing the system against a hand-derived result, and never on the strength of what a document asserted about itself. Where the checked-in documentation and the running system disagreed, the running system was taken as correct and the discrepancy recorded.
