## 1. System purpose and domain

Resultiq automates the complete result-analysis workflow of a single engineering college affiliated with DBATU (Dharmsinh Desai University of Technology). It takes raw student marks — arriving as printed result statements in spreadsheets, text-layer PDFs and scanned image PDFs — and turns them into accurate, auditable, role-governed results, analytics, topper lists and downloadable Excel reports.

The problem, in the project's own terms: manual analysis of a 100-plus-student class takes two to three days, produces human calculation errors, has no standardised handling of the university's three curriculum schemes, identifies toppers incorrectly (especially ties), produces inconsistent reports, and offers no access control at all, so a user sees either everything or nothing.

**The academic domain.** The system models the real structure of an Indian engineering college:

- **Branches** — the engineering departments. Five seeded: Computer Science & Engineering (CS), Mechanical (ME), Civil (CE), Electrical (EE), Electronics & Telecommunication (ENT).
- **Schemes** — curriculum revisions. Three seeded: 2019, 2022, 2024, each with its own rule document and effective-from date.
- **Academic years** — calendar year with start/end dates and an active flag. One seeded: 2024-25, 1 July 2024 to 30 June 2025.
- **Batches** — student cohort, tied to the year of admission. One seeded, the 2024-25 cohort.
- **Semesters** — numbered 1 to 8. A "class" is one branch's one semester's one scheme's one academic year.
- **Subjects** — subject identity: code, name, credits, maximum marks, aggregate passing marks, and an assessment component breakdown (two continuous assessments, mid-semester, written, practical, oral).
- **Subject offerings** — a subject as it actually runs, assigned to a branch, semester, scheme and year. The unit marks hang off and the unit a teacher is allocated.
- **Examinations** — a scheduled examination for a class context, with a status tracking the result from pending through processing to published.
- **Students** — roll number, name, branch, semester, scheme, batch, academic year, plus personal, contact, category and guardian details, and an optional link to a login account.

**Users.** Six roles (Section 4): a Super Administrator who configures the college and audits everything but cannot write academic data; an Administrator who is the examination cell and owns students, subjects and marks; a Head of Department who reads and allocates within one branch; a Principal who reads and supervises with no write rights at all; a Teacher who enters marks only for allocated subjects; and a Student who sees only their own result.

**Audience principle.** The stated audience is explicitly not developers. Every capability must be reachable by clicking through the interface, not only by calling an API. Raw JSON, internal identifiers, enum values, permission strings and developer labels must never appear in the interface.

**Scope boundary.** One college. Multi-college or tenant isolation is an explicit non-goal; branch is the partitioning unit.

## 2. The problem it solves

**Speed.** A 100-plus-student class takes two to three days by hand. The system ingests an entire printed result statement in one upload, parses it into rows, requires human confirmation, and writes the marks in one transaction — reducing the cycle to minutes.

**Arithmetic accuracy.** Hand re-derivation of totals, percentages, credits and verdicts produces errors. The system replaces hand arithmetic with three scheme-aware calculators hard-coded to the university's thresholds, returning full precision (rounding happens only at display). It re-derives the college's own summary columns — total marks, percentage, SGPA, result — rather than trusting them, because re-deriving them is the point.

**Scheme standardisation.** Three curriculum schemes run simultaneously with different component structures and pass rules. Manual handling of all three is error-prone, so each scheme is a separate calculator strategy, selected automatically by the scheme on the student's record.

**Topper identification.** Manual topper lists mishandle ties, absence and withheld results. The system detects a tie explicitly and lists every student at the top mark at rank 1, excludes absent and withheld marks from every statistic, and offers three ranking rules (percentage, credits, raw marks), each with a deterministic tie-break chain.

**Access control and auditability.** Historically every user saw everything or nothing, with no record of who changed what. The system enforces permission-based authorisation with branch scoping and allocation-based ownership, and writes an append-only trail of every academic mutation: who did it, what they changed, why, from which address, with what client.

**A sixth, softer problem: the "pending vs failed" error.** Manual practice treats a missing mark as a zero, reporting that every student failed. The system treats a subject with no marks as *pending* — no verdict yet — and refuses to publish a report over an incomplete class. This is the most important rule in the computation (Section 6).

## 3. Complete feature inventory

Grouped by functional area. Each entry gives what the feature does and who uses it.

### 3.1 Identity and access — *all roles for own account; Super Administrator for members, roles, invitations, audit*
Email/password sign-in returning a short-lived access token, a longer-lived refresh token, the full role list and the union of permissions; silent session refresh with token rotation; sign out and sign out everywhere. Forgotten password: reset link emailed on request, identical response whether or not the address is registered, most recent link only, 30-minute expiry. Change own password, open to everyone. Forced-password-change server guard blocks every authenticated route while a change is pending. Profile: display name and picture editable, email deliberately not self-service-editable. Member directory with search, role/branch filters, view, update, activate, archive (archiving and revocation are the same act). Impact preview reports what a member holds before removal; revocation cascades to tokens, allocations, roles and outstanding invitations, leaving the affected class flagged as needing a class teacher. Invitation lifecycle: invite a named person to a named role in a named branch, one-time acceptance link, 168-hour (7-day) expiry, revocable, resend supersedes rather than leaving two live links. Role catalogue and permission matrix: roles as cards with descriptions, active status, member and permission counts; editable matrix with tri-state group checkboxes and a live "N of M selected" counter; separate permission catalogue; arbitrary custom roles alongside the six system roles. Read-only audit trail filterable by action, resource and member, with dropdown values from a server-side facet aggregation. Per-member in-app notifications with unread filtering, mark-one, mark-all and delete.

### 3.2 Academic configuration — *Super Administrator: branches, years, schemes, offerings; Super Administrator and HOD: subjects; HOD: allocations; Examination Cell: subjects, offerings, examinations*
Branches: create, rename, deactivate, delete. A branch is the scoping unit for the whole system — role assignment, offerings, allocations, class teachers and the scheme reader all hang off it. Academic years: listed read-only with no create button, because the backend exposes no create endpoint and a button that cannot save would ship a broken feature; the write permission exists in the catalogue but no screen uses it. Batches: no administration screen — a batch appears only as a selection on the student screens. Schemes: card grid with name, description, effective-from date, active status and a collapsible rule document. Subjects: catalogue of identities with an assessment template; the components column shows the first three plus a "+N more" indicator; two tabs (catalogue, offerings by semester across the eight semesters); branch/scheme/semester filters are placement filters resolved through the offerings list, because a subject row carries no branch. Offerings: placing a subject into a class context; a separate record with a separate permission and a different owner; duplicate placement in the same class context is refused, since it would silently split one class's marks across two rows. Allocations: binding an offering to a teacher for a year from a branch-scoped list, not the whole member directory; removal warns the teacher will lose marks entry. Class-teacher assignment: exactly one per class context, enforced by a uniqueness constraint, replaced via remove-then-create.

### 3.3 Students — *Examination Cell (the only role that adds students); HOD read within branch; Student own record only*
Roster with pagination and filters on branch, scheme, semester, year and free text; view, update, archive. Every write is checked against branch scope, including the branch a student is being moved *into*, so the edit form cannot transfer a student across branches. Single creation with identity, contact, category and guardian data. Bulk import from a spreadsheet: header row must carry roll number and name (aliases matched loosely); branch, scheme, year, batch and semester are chosen once and applied to every row, and any class column in the sheet is ignored rather than allowed to override, so nobody files students under the wrong branch. No preview or dry-run — one request. Per-row errors reported with 1-based row numbers that include the header row, so data starts at row 2. Over 5,000 data rows: only the first 5,000 import and it says so. Caps 5 MB / 5,000 rows. Curriculum year derived from semester and shown disabled, so the two can never disagree. Roll-number uniqueness is checked server-side only, surfacing as "That already exists". Login provisioning individually or per class, linking each student record to its owning account. Student profile with academic snapshot, marks history, result history, documents and activity trail.

### 3.4 Marks and evaluation — *Teacher for allocated subjects only; Examination Cell for any subject in the class*
Manual single entry (one mark per student, offering, component and year; re-saving updates rather than duplicating). Manual bulk entry via a grid of students against one offering and component: only touched cells are sent, so saving never blanks untouched components; a bulk write touching an unallocated subject is rejected whole, because "47 of 50 saved" is never acceptable. Absence marking stores zero obtained plus an absence flag, keeping absence distinguishable from a genuine zero. A withheld flag excludes a result from every statistic, breakdown and report. A separate correction route lets the Examination Cell overwrite a teacher's mark, requiring the exam-cell write permission and a written reason, recorded under its own audit action, preserving the teacher's original. Result computation yields per student: component totals, aggregate, percentage, credits earned, pass/fail and completeness, with an explicit evaluated flag so "not entered yet" is distinguishable from "Fail". Credits earned count only passed subjects; total credits count all offered subjects. Student totals span the student's own class — subjects actually offered to that branch, semester and scheme for the year, not the whole catalogue. Staff reaching a student's marks through the roster see the grid pre-seeded with what is on record.

### 3.5 File upload and OCR — *Teacher and Examination Cell upload; both confirm, with different reach*
Formats accepted: PDF (text-layer and scanned), Excel, CSV, plain text. Type decided from the **bytes** via magic-byte signatures, extension only a last-resort fallback; PDFs then classified text-based or image-only by an image marker in the header. 50 MB cap, enforced at the HTTP layer and in the service. Two entry points: register a file already placed in storage by URL, or upload bytes directly; the URL route checks the address against the server-side-fetch guard before anything is written or queued, so a refused address leaves nothing behind. Processing runs on background queues with pollable status: pending, processing, extracted, completed, failed, the message stored on the job.

*Printed result statement parser* (the format the examination cell actually receives): a wide table, one row per student, merged two-row header, subject blocks separated by a pipe. Because the table is laid out in columns rather than delimited, cell boundaries come from runs of two or more spaces. It deliberately handles the awkward parts — a subject code is recognised by shape so "Roll", "Percentage" and "SGPA" are never mistaken for a subject heading (which would shift every later column out of alignment); the college's own summary columns are ignored and re-derived; the practical block is kept separate from the theory block of the same subject so a practical recorded under the theory's component names cannot silently overwrite the theory's mark; default maxima are used because the statement prints marks and never maxima; "Absent" is zero with the flag while "Fail" is zero without it; a **blank cell is not a zero** — it means the component was not conducted, so the omission is reported and the component left out, because recording it as zero would silently fail the student; an over-maximum mark is kept and reported later at the step that knows the real maximum; a row with no roll number is an error rather than a guess; a file naming no subject is rejected outright as not a printed result statement.

*Tidy sheet extractor* handles the long layout (one row per mark) with generous header aliases, capped at 5,000 rows. *PDF text extractor* attempts a line-based table parse (rows by newline; columns by runs of two-plus spaces, tabs or pipes); the code itself calls this best-effort. *OCR* selects among three Hugging Face models by content hint — table transformer, handwriting recogniser, general-purpose OCR — with table detection as a second optional call whose failure is logged without failing the extraction. Recognised text is fed through the same tidy-sheet parser, so the OCR review screen has the same shape as a spreadsheet upload; if no header naming both a roll number and a subject is found, the user is told to upload a spreadsheet instead.

**Mandatory human review:** nothing is written to the marks table automatically; the job sits in the extracted state until a person reviews the rows and confirms. **Two-stage confirmation:** the exam cell's right covers every offering in the class; the teacher's covers only their allocations for the year. A row the teacher is not allocated to is **skipped, not rejected**, because a statement commonly covers the whole class — the upload still completes with skipped rows listed. Confirmation rules: branch, scheme, semester and year must all be in the upload metadata; the confirmer must be permitted to write in the named branch (the branch is a filter, never a grant); students are resolved by roll number **within branch and year**, because a roll number is only unique inside a branch; the subject's configured maximum wins over the parser default, and an over-maximum mark is reported and skipped rather than written, because it would corrupt every percentage derived from it; all writes in one transaction. Row-level problems — unreadable values, missing components, unknown students, subjects not offered, unallocated subjects, over-maximum marks — are collected with reasons rather than thrown.

### 3.6 Scheme reading (curriculum PDF to subjects) — *Examination Cell and HOD*
A three-step flow with a human in the middle; the proposal is never written straight through. Scope (branch, scheme, year) must be complete before upload, and changing any scope selection clears the reading, so a stale proposal is never carried over. A format warning states before upload that the PDF must be a text PDF — a photograph of a printed page will not work.

**The design principle is explicit: the code selects, the model interprets.** A deterministic parser for DBATU table geometry was built and abandoned, because the document family is unbounded and rule-based reading fails silently with plausible-but-wrong numbers. The deterministic layer is kept only for *selection* — locate the course table and reduce it to a positional slice. The model reads it: a GROQ reader model returns structured course rows at temperature 0 under a strict schema, and a separate guard model inspects the document for prompt-injection attempts **before** the reader sees it, its verdict surfaced to the reviewer. The backend validates every row — components must sum to 100, hours present, totals row excluded, semester read from the heading above the table.

The review panel reports how many subjects were read from how many pages, how many semesters were found, elapsed time, a document safety verdict (safe, partly checked, refused) naming the worst guard score and unchecked pages, and the count of dropped rows with the first six reasons — **dropped rows are shown, not hidden**. Warnings name subject, message and page. The course table is fully editable (code, name, type, semester, lecture hours, practical hours, continuous assessment, mid-semester, end-semester, credits, source page) and collapses to labelled cards on mobile. An empty number cell posts as **absent**, not zero — "the document did not say" differs from "worth zero". Before the write, counts are shown: how many subjects will be saved and how many skipped; an existing subject is updated rather than duplicated, and a course is skipped when it has no code or its marks total zero. If the AI service is unavailable the module still boots, the upload area is replaced by a message offering manual entry, and the same review table can be filled in by hand — **the AI is an accelerator, never a hard dependency**. Course types are spelled out in full: Basic Science, Engineering Science, Professional Core, Vocational Skill Enhancement, Ability Enhancement, Co-Curricular, Indian Knowledge System, Multidisciplinary Elective, Mandatory Indian Course. A CC row's option choice is recorded per student, not per offering.

### 3.7 Result viewing and student self-view — *Student; staff via the roster*
**My Result**: identity block (name, roll number, academic year, publication date or status); four summary cards (percentage with marks obtained out of total, pass/fail verdict with a count of subjects still to clear, credits earned out of total, subjects graded with the mark-entry count beneath); a "subjects to clear" panel listing each failed subject with its reason; a subject-wise table; a component-wise breakdown with per-entry status badges (entered, absent, withheld); and the class leaderboard with the signed-in student's own row shaded and badged. Three independently paginated tables. **Publication gating:** a result is visible only once an examination has been scheduled for the class *and* published; otherwise the student is told the result has not been declared, rather than shown a zeroed marksheet that reads like a failure. **Identity resolution:** the student is resolved from the token, with no client-supplied identifier, so a student cannot ask for anyone else's result. The student dashboard is materially different from an administrator's — scope resolves to "own", and the recent-uploads section is not rendered at all rather than shown empty.

### 3.8 Analytics — *Examination Cell and Principal college-wide; HOD own department; Teacher own subjects*
Overview: total students, total offerings, total mark records, overall pass percentage, average percentage. Pass/fail statistics computed **per student**, not per mark — each student's marks aggregated into one percentage, then banded. Subject statistics: students, average marks, average percentage, highest and lowest mark, pass and fail counts, pass percentage, and a difficulty label from the pass rate. Performance trends: one point per academic year, oldest first — average percentage, pass percentage, student count — with years carrying no marks still appearing, with zeroes. Filters: academic year (explicit, or the active year resolved automatically), branch (one or many), scheme, semester; marks carry no branch of their own, so branch filtering runs through the student relation for student-scope queries and the offering relation for subject-scope queries. Absent and withheld marks excluded from every figure.

### 3.9 Reports — *Examination Cell and HOD generate/download; Principal read/download; Super Administrator read*
Seven types: student-wise result, scheme-wise marks, subject analysis, student analysis, class toppers, pass/fail analysis, and a **complete** report bundling everything. **Excel only**, produced with ExcelJS; there is no PDF report and no print-specific output anywhere in the backend. The complete report has seven sheets: Student Wise Result, Scheme Wise Marks, Subject Analysis, Student Analysis, Class Toppers, Pass/Fail Analysis, Charts Data. Each row carries the calculated percentage, the pass verdict, credits earned against total credits, and a completeness flag, so the sheet can never print a verdict the student did not earn; the scheme-aware calculator for the class's scheme is used. **The readiness gate:** a report is a published statement about the class, so generation is **refused while any student's result is incomplete**. The refusal names what is missing — how many students complete, how many pending, which subjects are missing (with how many students missing each), and which students are pending (capped at 20 named, count beside them); a separate readiness check tells the operator what is missing *before* pressing generate. Generation is queued, falling back to inline execution if the queue is unavailable so the job is never lost. The download **regenerates the file fresh from current marks** rather than serving a stored copy, so the readiness check runs again at download time — marks may have been added or withdrawn since queuing, and a download must never hand out a sheet stating a verdict for a result that is not in. Scope applies throughout: a branch-scoped member may only generate, read, download and delete reports for their own branches, and deletion is checked with the same scope as reading, otherwise the read scope would be theatre.

### 3.10 Toppers — *Examination Cell, HOD, Principal, Teacher (within branch scope); Student own class only*
**Subject-wise**: for every subject in the class, the highest-scoring student or students, with mark, percentage and whether the top mark is a tie; a tie is detected explicitly and every student at the top mark is listed at rank 1; the score is the sum of that subject's components. **Class toppers**: leaderboard with each student's total marks, total possible marks, percentage and credits earned, computed through the scheme-aware calculator. **Class toppers by rule**: three ranking strategies, each a pure, separately testable algorithm — *percentage* (overall percentage, ties broken by credits, then total marks, then roll number); *credits* (credits earned first as a proxy for passing more subjects, then percentage, then total marks, then roll number); *marks* (raw total marks obtained, falling back to percentage, then roll number). **Student-facing**: a student sees the leaderboard of **their own class only**, derived entirely from their own record so it cannot be widened with query parameters.

### 3.11 Class overview — *class teacher (HOD or Teacher holding the assignment)*
A class teacher's view of their own class. Classes are listed from their own class-teacher assignments, and a class is identified by the **assignment identifier** rather than by branch and semester, so the client cannot address a class the teacher does not hold. Headline statistics: student count, subject count, average, highest and lowest percentage, students with complete marks, students with no marks at all, students with missing marks, passed and failed counts. The pass/fail split is decided **per student**, so a student who failed two subjects counts once; students with no marks at all are counted separately so the pass and fail figures never double-count. Per-subject standing: students with marks, average percentage, highest mark and who achieved it, pass count. Per-student roster: total marks, percentage, subjects with marks, missing subjects, failed subjects, standing.

### 3.12 Dashboard — *all roles, at their own scope*
Permission-driven, resolving one of four scopes before computing anything. **Own**: a member with a linked student record sees only their own result — overall percentage, verdict, subjects graded, roll number, subject-wise performance chart. **Class**: a member who can enter marks only for their own allocations and holds no exam-cell write right (the teacher); their world narrows to the offerings they are allocated and the classes they are class teacher for, and a teacher with no allocation gets an **empty** dashboard, not the whole college. **Branch**: a member who can read own-department analytics (HOD). **College**: a member who can read analytics across the college (Principal, exam-cell Administrator), still narrowed to their branch scope if they hold one. Metrics: total students, total offerings, pass percentage, average percentage, and — for those who can act on it or supervise it — marks uploaded and marks pending; the upload-pipeline metrics are withheld from the Principal deliberately, because they hold no write permission and could do nothing about a pending count. Chart sections: students per branch (college scope only), students per semester, subject offerings per semester, pass percentage per branch, average performance by academic year, toppers per branch, branches with missing data (governance view, college scope only), subject offerings awaiting marks (a to-do list), result publication status per semester. Two design points: the trend line aggregates **per student** first, because averaging mark rows would weight a student with more components more heavily; and the publication-status query is wrapped in a guard because the examinations table may not be migrated yet, so that when it fails the dashboard still renders and reports the omission as a degraded item rather than showing a misleading zero.

### 3.13 Audit — *Super Administrator and Principal read*
Every academic mutation is recorded: who, what, when, the entity changed and its identifier, free-form details including a **reason** where the operation warrants one, and request context (address, client fingerprint, method, route). A fixed, filterable action vocabulary — create, update, delete, bulk import, confirm, correction, grant, revoke, deactivate, archive. Corrections get their own action rather than being filed as an ordinary update, because a correction overwrites work another person is responsible for; privilege changes get their own actions because they are the most consequential thing the system records. Resources covered: student, subject, offering, mark, branch, scheme, allocation, class-teacher assignment, upload job, user. Beyond audited routes the system records logins, password changes, profile updates, avatar changes, invitation acceptances, password-reset requests, role grants and revocations, and member deactivations. Read scoping: a college-scoped reader sees everything; a branch-scoped reader sees only entries by members of their branch, plus their own always; facets are scoped identically, so a dropdown never offers a value the list would refuse. Write policy: audit rows go through a single service that never throws — a failed write is logged and dropped, because the mutation has already committed and failing the request afterwards would be worse than a missing row — and the write is awaited before the response returns, so a shutdown cannot lose rows.

### 3.14 Notifications and email — *all roles receive; Examination Cell creates notifications*
In-app notifications per member: create, list (unread-only filter), mark one read, mark all read, delete, all owner-checked. Two emails, both queued through SMTP — the **invitation email** (invitee's name, role, one-time acceptance link, expiry date) and the **password-reset email** (a reset link with an expiry timestamp). Both render from a shared HTML layout with every interpolated value escaped, and the invite URL escaped separately for HTML output. **No temporary password is ever generated or sent** — the invitee sets their own password on acceptance. The API never blocks on an SMTP round trip: if the queue is unavailable the email is sent inline, so an invitation is never silently dropped because Redis was down. When SMTP is unconfigured the module still boots, outgoing email is disabled, and the system logs that the link must be shared manually. Only the recipient and the message identifier are logged — never the body, never a token inside it.

### 3.15 Administration
Members (three tabs — Members, Invitations, Roles), Roles and Permissions (five panels), Branches, Academic Years, Subjects, Subject Offerings, Schemes, Scheme Reader, Assignments (two tabs — Subject allocations, Class teachers), Audit Log. Member creation is **invite-only**; there is no "create a user with a password" path. Editing a member changes only name and email — roles are managed on a separate panel, because a role change must go through the RBAC endpoints so the permission cache is invalidated and the change is audited.

## 4. Roles and permissions

**The model.** Permission-based, with roles as named bundles. There is deliberately **no role guard** in the route layer. Every route declares the permission or permissions it requires; role names appear only in the seed's mapping, the grant-authority rule and display labels. The guard, the authorisation service and the client session all read the same single implementation of the effective-permission join, so they can never disagree. A member may hold **several roles at once**; the effective set is the union. The token carries one primary role for display, but every authorisation decision reads the full union from the database through the cache. Enforcement requires **all** listed permissions on a route, not any one — except where two rights can legitimately land on the same operation, in which case the route lists a common permission and the service resolves which specific right the caller holds. That is the case for confirming an upload, where the exam cell's administrative right and the teacher's allocation-limited right are deliberately distinct.

**Permission shape.** Each permission is a resource-and-action pair. **45 are seeded across 20 resources**: students (read, read own, write); marks (read, read own, write, enter own subject); reports (read, generate, download); upload (create, read, process, process own subject); analytics (read, read own department); users (read, write); rbac (read, write); audit (read); invites (read, write); dashboard (read); branches (read, write, delete); schemes (read, write); subjects (read, write, delete); toppers (read, read own class); subject offerings (read, write); academic years (read, write); examinations (read, write); teacher allocations (read, write); class teacher assignments (read, write); notifications (write). The narrower `_own` and `_own_subject` variants exist so a narrower right can be granted without a full resource-wide permission.

**The six roles**, exactly as named in code: **SUPER_ADMIN, ADMIN, HOD, PRINCIPAL, TEACHER, STUDENT**, with seeded counts of 22, 25, 20, 12, 16 and 4 respectively — 99 bindings in total.

**SUPER_ADMIN — 22 permissions. Configuration and governance, VIEW-ONLY on academic data.** Can: manage members, roles, permissions, invitations, branches, academic years, schemes and offerings; read students, marks, reports, analytics, toppers, dashboard and audit trail. Cannot: create students, enter or edit marks, upload files, or generate reports — it holds **no** student-write, **no** marks-write and **no** upload-create permission. Deliberate: Super Admin is the configuration role, not the operational one, so the person who configures the college cannot quietly alter its academic data. Never grantable through the application, only by the system administrator.

**ADMIN (examination cell) — 25 permissions. The operational role.** Can: create students single and bulk; create, edit and delete subjects, templates and offerings; enter marks for every subject in the class; upload files and approve result extractions for the whole batch; generate and download reports; read full-college analytics and toppers; write examinations and publish notifications. Cannot: manage members, roles, permissions, branches or schemes; and holds no allocation of its own, so the allocation-limited upload-processing right is deliberately absent. The division of labour matters: **the examination cell defines what a subject is worth; the teacher fills in what each student scored.**

**HOD — 20 permissions. A branch-scoped reader plus allocator.** Can: read students, marks, reports, analytics, toppers and uploads for their branch; generate and download reports for their branch; create and edit subjects and offerings for their branch; allocate teaching and class teachers within their branch; read the own-department analytics right that puts them in the branch-scope dashboard. Cannot: create students, enter marks for arbitrary subjects, invite users, configure permissions, or reach beyond their own branch. The two allocation duties are separate permissions, so one HOD can allocate subjects while another handles class-teacher duties.

**PRINCIPAL — 12 permissions. Strictly read-only, ZERO write permissions.** Can: read students, marks, reports (and download them), full-college analytics, dashboard, branches, schemes, subjects, offerings, toppers and the audit trail. Cannot: upload, create students, write marks, generate reports, write allocations, or publish notifications. **The Principal holds not a single write permission of any kind.**

**TEACHER — 16 permissions.** Can: read students, marks, reports, uploads, branches, schemes, subjects, offerings, toppers and analytics; read their own allocations and class-teacher assignments; **enter marks only for allocated subjects** (the allocation check runs in the service layer against the year being written); **write extracted upload scores only for those same subjects**; create their own uploads. Cannot: create students, write marks for unallocated subjects, approve a whole-batch extraction, generate or download reports, or manage branches, schemes or allocations. The teacher holds the allocation-limited marks and upload-processing permissions, **not** the general write ones.

**STUDENT — 4 permissions. Own result only.** Can: see the dashboard shell, their own student record, their own marks, and their own class's toppers. Cannot: see anything else. The student deliberately does **not** hold the general student-list or marks-read permission, because those would expose the whole roster and the whole mark sheet. Requests for another student's record are refused.

**Branch scoping.** Isolation is branch-based, derived from the member's role-assignment rows rather than the token, because a person can hold roles in several branches and the union decides what they see. A member with at least one branch-bearing assignment is **branch-scoped** and every read is filtered to those branches; a member whose assignments all name no branch is **college-scoped**. A branch-scoped member requesting a branch outside their scope is **refused, not silently redirected** — rejection makes the mismatch visible rather than hiding a client bug. A member scoped to several branches who names none still sees only those branches: scope is a **set**, not a single identifier, and a single-identifier implementation would drop the filter entirely and show the whole college. Reads and writes are separated — a college-scoped member may read any branch but is still bounded on writes to the class being worked on. Scope is applied in the **service layer**, not only in controller decorators, because a decorator alone is bypassable by any code path that calls the service directly — a background job, a queue processor — so every marks-write path goes through one gate and no path can drift. Scope is derived from the caller and never accepted as a request parameter.

**Grant authority.** A member may grant a **staff** role only if it carries strictly less authority than the strongest role they hold. A member holding several roles is judged by the **strongest**, and the comparison is strict, so nobody may appoint a peer — an Administrator cannot appoint another Administrator, a Principal cannot appoint another Principal. The Student role is **appointed** rather than granted as staff: it may be handed out from Principal level upward, since appointing a student is not a teacher's act.

**Ownership and allocation.** Beyond permission and scope, a third check applies at record level: the caller must be responsible for that specific record. A teacher may write marks only for offerings allocated to them for the year being written. The exam cell may write any offering in scope **except** one a teacher holds — overwriting requires an explicit, audited correction carrying a written reason, recorded under its own audit action rather than as an ordinary entry. A mark's student must belong to the offering's branch, and an upload's row must resolve to a student in the branch named in the upload metadata and to a subject actually offered in that class.

## 5. Key business workflows

**(a) Login and session.** The user enters an email and a password; both are checked for emptiness on submit with inline field errors, and only the credentials go over the wire. The server verifies the password against a bcrypt hash, checks the account is active, checks no password change is pending, and issues a short-lived access token plus a longer-lived refresh token with the member's primary role, full role list and union of permissions. The client stores the session and redirects to the dashboard, a requested destination, or straight to the change-password screen if the account must change its password. A rate-limit response states how many minutes remain in the retry window. On every subsequent request the access token travels in the authorization header; if expired the client silently refreshes, queues concurrent requests behind the single refresh in flight, stores the rotated refresh token and replays the queued requests; if the refresh fails the session is cleared and the browser is sent to login. There is no "remember me" control — the session lives in local storage regardless.

**(b) Forgot and reset password.** The user submits an address; the server issues a random token, stores only its SHA-256 hash, sets a 30-minute expiry and emails the link. The response text is identical whether or not the address is registered, so it cannot enumerate who has an account; only the most recent link remains valid. Opening the link with a missing token shows an invalid-link card with a button back to the request form. Otherwise the user chooses and confirms a new password — the mismatch check runs first, and the stated policy is at least 8 characters including an uppercase letter, a lowercase letter, a number and a special character. On success the user is sent to login with a success toast. Every request in this flow is audited.

**(c) Invitation and activation.** Member creation is invite-only; there is no path to create a user with a chosen password. An administrator enters a name, an email, a role and — only for branch-scoped roles — a branch, and the system creates an invitation and emails a one-time acceptance link naming the recipient, their role and the seven-day expiry. Super Admin is excluded from what can be invited. The acceptance link is also copied to the clipboard automatically, so an administrator can share it by hand if mail is not configured — because raw tokens are never persisted, copying actually **reissues** the link and revokes the old one, so exactly one is ever live. A resend marks the old invitation superseded, rendering as "Replaced"; an administrator can withdraw a pending invitation with a confirmation. Opening the link validates the token first, showing a spinner, then either a refusal card (invalid or already used) or a card showing the assigned name, email and role followed by password fields. On submit the account is created, the role and branch assigned, and the invitation consumed **in one transaction**, so the token cannot be replayed; the session is stored and the new member lands on the dashboard. Every invalid token produces one indistinguishable message.

**(d) User and role administration.** The Members screen lists each member with their email, role chips showing role and branch code, status including a "Password change pending" sub-line, last login (or "Never"), and actions to edit, activate/deactivate or remove. Editing changes only name and email. Roles are granted on a separate panel: "Add a role" opens a dialog listing only the roles **below the signed-in member's own authority**, with a branch dropdown appearing only for branch-scoped roles — for a college-wide role the dialog says so in words instead. A role already held college-wide is not offered again, but a role held in *another* branch is, which is how one teacher works in two branches. Taking a role back asks for confirmation and names the branch whose access will be lost; if the member holds no grantable role the panel says so and hides the add button. **The change takes effect on the member's next request — no re-login is needed** — because a global version counter is bumped, orphaning every cached permission set in constant time. Removing a member asks for confirmation in words, first reporting what they currently hold; on completion it releases their allocations and class-teacher assignment, revokes their tokens and outstanding invitations, and leaves their class visibly flagged as needing a class teacher.

**(e) Academic setup.** The Super Administrator creates branches, then academic years, then schemes with their pass rules. Subjects arrive through the scheme-reading workflow or by hand; the exam cell may then edit subject metadata and offerings. Each subject carries a unique code, credits, maximum marks, an aggregate passing mark and an assessment component breakdown, and is placed into a class context as an offering. The curriculum year is derived from the semester rather than entered, so the two can never contradict each other. The HOD then allocates subjects to teachers for the year and assigns exactly one class teacher per class context. Allocating checks that the teacher is active, the offering is active, there is no duplicate for the year, and the offering belongs to the **caller's** branch — so one branch's head cannot hand another branch's subject to their own teacher.

**(f) Student registration and bulk import.** The exam cell creates students one at a time or bulk-imports a spreadsheet. The sheet needs a header row with roll number and name (aliases matched loosely); branch, scheme, year, batch and semester are chosen once and applied to every row, and any class column in the sheet is ignored rather than allowed to override. The import is a single request with no preview; on success the inserted count is reported, and if rows failed the failure count is reported too with the first ten per-row errors listed by row number. Over 5,000 data rows, only the first 5,000 import and it says so. Every row's branch is checked even when the row names its own. Logins are provisioned individually or per class, with each student record linked to the owning account.

**(g) Marks entry.** The teacher opens the marks screen in the class context and sees only the subjects allocated to them. They enter component-wise scores or mark a student absent, and save. Only touched cells are sent, so saving never blanks components they did not open; an absent cell is sent as zero obtained with the absent flag; a non-numeric value is skipped rather than coerced; and changing the class clears the subject selection, so a grid can never show one class's students against another class's subject. The write passes through one shared authorisation gate in the service layer, and a bulk write touching an unallocated subject is rejected whole. The exam cell may write any offering in its scope except one a teacher holds — overwriting requires an explicit, reasoned, audited correction preserving the teacher's original entry.

**(h) File upload to written marks.** The user picks the class in the shared class bar and drops an Excel, CSV or PDF file; the client checks extension and MIME type first and refuses anything else with a message naming what is expected, but the server decides the type from the bytes. The file is written to storage and a background job is queued; the screen polls every two seconds while pending or processing, showing live status text. The server fetches the file (from local storage directly, or over HTTP for files elsewhere — only after the address guard passes), detects the type from the magic bytes, classifies a PDF as text-based or image-only, and parses it by whichever route applies. The extracted rows are written onto the job with row-level errors collected rather than thrown. Nothing is written to the marks table at this point — the job sits in the extracted state. A member with the upload-processing right sees a "Confirm and save" button; a member with only upload rights sees an explanatory line instead of a button that would fail. On confirmation the rows are written per offering in one transaction: the confirmer's reach is checked (the exam cell's covers every offering in the class; the teacher's covers only their allocations, with unallocated rows **skipped and listed** rather than rejected); students are resolved by roll number within branch and year; the subject's configured maximum overrides the parser default, and an over-maximum mark is reported and skipped; and re-confirming updates rather than duplicating, because the write is keyed on student, offering, component and year. The user is then pointed to the student list.

**(i) Scheme PDF reading.** The exam cell sets the scope and uploads the syllabus PDF; changing any scope selection clears the reading. The document is extracted locally and rasterised. A guard model screens it for prompt-injection instructions and its verdict is recorded. The deterministic layer selects the course table and reduces it to a positional slice; the reader model interprets that slice into structured course rows at temperature 0 under a strict schema. The backend validates every row. The review panel reports how many subjects were read from how many pages, how many semesters were found, elapsed time, the document safety verdict, and the count of dropped rows with the first six reasons. The examiner corrects rows in an editable table and sees validation failures per row. Before the write the counts are shown. On confirmation, subjects and offerings are written with pass rules derived, an existing subject updated rather than duplicated, and a course skipped when it has no code or its marks total zero. If the AI service is unavailable the same review table can be filled in by hand.

**(j) Result viewing and student self-view.** The student opens My Result and sees the identity block, four summary cards, a subjects-to-clear panel if any subject failed, the subject-wise table, the component-wise breakdown with status badges, and the class leaderboard with their own row shaded and badged. The result is visible only once an examination for the class has been scheduled **and** published; before that the student is told the result has not been declared. The student is resolved from the token, so no other student's record can be requested. Staff reach a single student's marks through the roster, where the grid is pre-seeded with what is on record.

**(k) Analytics.** The user lands on a row of KPI cards (total students, pass percentage, average percentage, subjects) plus three charts, with filters above for branch, scheme and semester that start from the shared class but can be widened. Leaving the filters empty shows everything the member is entitled to see, because the server resolves the caller's branch scope itself rather than trusting the request. Charts appear immediately rather than requiring a selection step. Pass/fail statistics are computed per student and banded into distinction, first class, second class, borderline and fail; subject statistics carry a difficulty label from the pass rate; trends show one point per academic year including years with no marks.

**(l) Report generation.** The user picks the class in the shared bar and selects a report type. Before the generate button becomes usable, the application asks the server whether the class's result is complete. If it is, a green notice confirms every student's result is in. If not, the screen explains exactly what is missing — the subjects with marks outstanding and how many students each is missing for, and, in a collapsible list, the students still awaiting marks and which subjects each is missing — and the button stays disabled with the reason on screen rather than a dead control. On generation the job is queued; the list of recent reports polls every three seconds while any report is queued or generating, shows status badges, and offers a download button that only enables on a completed report. The download writes a fresh Excel file named after the report identifier.

**(m) Toppers.** Two tabs. Subject toppers list, for every subject in the class, the highest mark with a clear indication when it is a tie — every student at the top mark is listed at rank 1. Class toppers show the leaderboard with each student's total marks, total possible marks, percentage and credits, computed through the scheme-aware calculator, and offer a choice of ranking rule with a deterministic tie-break chain. Absent and withheld marks are excluded from every figure. A student sees only their own class's leaderboard.

## 6. Evaluation rules

**2019 scheme.** Components: two continuous assessments, a mid-semester paper, a written theory paper and, where applicable, the practical. Passing requires **all** of: each continuous assessment at least **6 of 20**; mid-semester at least **8 of 20**; internal total (both continuous assessments plus mid-semester) at least **30 of 60**; written paper at least **45 of 100**; practical at least **10 of 25** where applicable; aggregate at least **50%**.

**2022 scheme.** Theory 100 = internal 40 (class test 15, mid-semester 15, micro-project 10) plus external 60 (the written paper). Practical 25, assessed separately. Passing requires: internal at least **20 of 40**; external at least **30 of 60**; practical at least **13 of 25** where applicable; aggregate at least **50%**.

**2024 scheme.** Theory 100 = internal 40 (class test 1 of 15, mid-semester 15, quiz 10) plus external 60; practical 25, separate. **Thresholds identical to 2022**: internal 20 of 40, external 30 of 60, practical 13 of 25, aggregate 50%.

**The pending-vs-failed rule.** A subject with no marks has no verdict yet. It is **PENDING, not failed** — scoring it as zero would report every half-entered class as a class in which everybody failed. Each subject result carries an explicit evaluated flag, and the reason reads "Result not entered yet" rather than "Fail". Consequences, consistent across calculators, reports, dashboard and analytics: a pending subject earns no credits but is counted separately rather than treated as a failure; a student's result is **complete** only when every offered subject has a verdict; a student **passes overall** only when the result is complete and every subject passes; and **report generation is refused for an incomplete class**, naming exactly which subjects and which students are missing.

**Totals, percentage and credits.** Subject obtained is the sum of its components (an absent component contributes zero); subject maximum is the sum of component maxima; percentage is obtained divided by maximum. Student totals sum across the offerings of **their class** — subjects actually offered to that branch, semester and scheme for the year, not the whole catalogue. Overall percentage is student total obtained divided by total maximum. Credits earned count only passed subjects; total credits count all offered subjects. **The calculators do not round** — full precision is returned, rounded only for display.

**Absent and withheld.** An absent component contributes zero and is flagged, keeping absence distinguishable from a genuine zero. Absent components do **not** trigger the "below minimum" reasons — a student absent for the written paper is reported as absent rather than as having failed the external threshold, though the aggregate still counts the zero. A withheld result is excluded from every statistic, breakdown and report.

**Grade bands** (analytics layer; the calculator has no letter grades): distinction at 75% and above; first class at 60% and above; second class at 50% and above; a borderline "ATKT" band at 40% and above that still counts as a pass; fail below 40%. **Subject difficulty labels**, from the pass rate: 70% and above easy, 50% and above moderate, below that hard.

**What does not exist:** no best-of rule (every component contributes); no attendance model (attendance is not a mark component and does not affect pass/fail); no re-evaluation workflow beyond the withheld flag and the audited correction route; no re-totalling or grace marks.

## 7. Data model

**Shape.** 23 entities, 1 enum, 8 explicit join or assignment tables, 31 secondary indexes, and 14 compound or single unique constraints beyond the primary keys. UUID primary keys are generated in the application.

**The entities, grouped.** *Identity and access (8):* User (a member of the college — staff or a student with a provisioned login — carrying the credential hash, activation state, last-login time, avatar pointer, invitation provenance and a forced-password-change flag); Role (a named bundle of authority); Permission (an atomic ability as a resource plus an action); UserRole (assignment of a role to a user, optionally narrowed to one branch, and the carrier of branch scoping and grant provenance); RolePermission (assignment of a permission to a role); RefreshToken (a hashed long-lived session token with expiry, revocation and a replaced-by pointer); PasswordReset (a one-time reset request stored only as a SHA-256 hash); Invite (no account exists until it is accepted, so an unaccepted invitation grants no access at all). *Academic structure (9):* Branch (the primary horizontal partition of the data); Scheme (a curriculum revision with a JSON rule document); AcademicYear (a calendar year with start/end dates and an active flag); Batch (a cohort tied to the year of admission); Subject (a subject identity, independent of any branch, semester, scheme or year); SubjectOffering (a subject's assignment to a class context — the row marks and teacher allocations hang off); Examination (a scheduled examination with a status tracking the result lifecycle plus a publication timestamp); ClassTeacherAssignment (the single class teacher for a class context); TeacherSubject (allocation of a teacher to an offering for a year). *Students and marks (2):* Student; Mark (one student's score for one offering in one component in one year — the central fact table). *Operational (4):* UploadJob (a trackable file-processing job with status, progress and extracted payload); Report (a report request with type, filters, output location and status); AuditLog (an append-only trail); Notification (a per-member message with read state).

**The single enum.** Only one is declared: **InviteStatus**, with four values — pending (sent, not yet accepted, and the only status that can be accepted), accepted, revoked, expired (flipped lazily when the invite is next read). **Every other status-like column is a plain string** — the status on examinations, upload jobs and reports; the assessment-component type on marks; the role name on invitations; the type on reports and notifications; the action on permissions and audit logs. The trade-off is deliberate in part — a string column absorbs a new value without a migration — but those vocabularies are enforced by application code, not the database.

**Relationships.** One-to-many: User to role assignments, refresh tokens, password resets, audit rows, notifications, upload jobs, reports and sent invitations; Branch to offerings, examinations, class-teacher assignments, students, role assignments and invitations; Scheme to offerings, examinations, class-teacher assignments and students; Subject to offerings; Offering to marks and allocations; AcademicYear to batches, students, marks, allocations, offerings, examinations and class-teacher assignments; Batch to students; Student to marks; Role to role assignments and permission grants; Permission to permission grants. One-to-one: Student to User — at most one student profile per account and one account per student. Explicit join tables: User to Role, carrying the optional branch scope and grant provenance, making it a ternary relation in practice; Role to Permission; Teacher to Offering via the allocation table, keyed by teacher, offering and year — the allocation gating a teacher's right to enter marks; Teacher to class context via the class-teacher table, keyed by branch, semester, scheme and year with the uniqueness constraint capping it at one teacher. Self-relation: Invitation to Invitation via a superseded-by pointer, forming the resend chain.

**The 14 unique constraints, by purpose.** *Identity uniqueness:* one account per email address; one role, permission, scheme and subject per name; one branch per short code. *Token uniqueness:* unique token hashes on refresh tokens, password resets and invitations, which both prevents duplicates and makes lookup a single index probe. *Grant uniqueness:* one holder of a given role per branch; no duplicate permission grant; one student profile per account; optional student login email unique when present. *Domain uniqueness — the important ones:* one offering of a subject per class context; one examination per class context per year; **at most one class teacher per class context**; one allocation per teacher, subject and year; **roll numbers unique within an academic year, not globally**; the academic-year label unique; and the mark idempotency key (student, offering, component, year), which is what makes re-importing the same sheet safe — it cannot double-count.

**The 31 indexes, by purpose.** Every foreign key traversed on a hot path is indexed; every status column driving a queue is indexed; every "list by parent, newest first" pattern gets a compound index whose leading column is the parent foreign key. Grouped: *login and session lookup* — email on users, active-state on users, token hash on refresh tokens; *per-user fan-out* — user identifier on refresh tokens, password resets, role assignments, notifications, upload jobs and reports; *permission resolution* — role identifier on role assignments and permission grants; *class-context lookup* — branch + scheme + semester on offerings, academic year on offerings/examinations/class-teacher assignments, teacher identifier on class-teacher assignments and allocations ("everything this teacher is assigned" — the allocation check on the mark-entry path), offering identifier on allocations and marks; *student access* — roll number on students, branch + semester on students (the class-roster query, the most frequent student listing), scheme on students, student identifier on marks ("all marks of one student"); *queue and job reads* — status + creation time on upload jobs, status on reports; *audit and notification reads* — user + creation time on audit logs, resource + resource identifier on audit logs, user + read-state + creation time on notifications; *lifecycle sweeps* — expiry on password resets, status + expiry on invitations, invited-by identifier on invitations, superseded-by pointer on invitations, email on invitations. Pattern invalidation uses cursor-based scanning, never the blocking key-listing command.

**Soft delete, deactivation, audit trail.** **There is no soft delete in the current schema** — no table carries a deletion timestamp. **Deactivation uses boolean active flags** on users, branches, schemes, subjects, offerings, academic years, batches and students: a logical deactivation hiding a record from normal reads while keeping its history and all related rows intact. The project guidance mandating soft deletes is not reflected in the schema. **The audit trail is a separate, append-only table**, recording actor, action, resource and identifier, a structured payload, the originating address, the client fingerprint and a creation timestamp. Rows are written by an interceptor applied only to routes that opt in, so read paths pay nothing. Two rules govern it: only successful mutations are recorded (a handler that throws changed nothing), and a failed audit write never fails the request — the mutation has already committed, so the failure is logged instead. The write is awaited before the response returns, so a shutdown cannot lose rows. **Timestamps:** every mutable business table carries a creation timestamp (database clock) and a modification timestamp; the larger academic tables additionally carry free-text creator and last-modifier identifiers — plain strings rather than foreign keys, so not referentially enforced, but a durable breadcrumb even if the referenced account is later removed. Four tables are deliberately timestamp-light — academic years, batches, permission grants and allocations — because they are reference facts rather than mutable records. **Token hygiene:** refresh, reset and invitation tokens are stored hashed, never in plaintext, so a database leak yields no usable credentials.

**Branch scoping in the data model.** Three mechanisms combine: the branch on the role-assignment row (a null branch means college-wide; scope is the union across assignment rows, read from the database per request rather than embedded in the token); the branch on the data rows; and the branch on the join tables. Scheme and academic year are the other two dimensions — a class context is the tuple of branch, semester, scheme and academic year, and exactly that tuple is what the uniqueness constraints on examinations and class-teacher assignments protect. Marks carry the academic year directly, so a score is always attributable to a specific year. **Curriculum year is derived, never stored:** an earlier version stored a year column on subject offerings and a current-year column on students; both were removed in the 2 November 2026 migration because the year is a function of the semester, and storing it twice allowed contradictory rows such as "semester 5, year 1" with nothing to reject them.

**Migration history.** Seven migrations in chronological order: an initial schema creating all tables; the invitation flow and member fields together with the invite-status enum; the password-reset table; the invitation supersession chain; the split of subjects into a subject identity and a subject-offerings assignment table; the user avatar URL; and the removal of the two redundant year columns. Two deserve note. **The subject-offering split (25 October 2026)** is the most significant change: the generated migration would have dropped the old branch, scheme, semester and year columns from subjects in the same statement that added the not-null replacement columns, destroying every existing row's data; it was hand-rewritten to be data-preserving — create and backfill the offer table first, add the new mark and allocation columns as nullable and backfill them, promote to not-null, then drop the old columns. Legacy marks that collapsed onto the same offering under the new key were deduplicated, keeping the most recent row of each group, and subject codes were made globally unique with deterministic suffixes for legacy duplicates. **The year-column removal (2 November 2026)** is the clearest expression of the principle that redundant derivable data should not be stored; the migration repairs any row whose stored year disagreed with its semester before dropping the columns.

**Expected scale for one college.** Roles 6 (fixed); permissions 45 (fixed, growing only with new abilities); permission grants roughly 99; branches 5 to 10; schemes 3; academic years 1 per year; batches 1 per admission year; users 50 to 300; role assignments roughly 1 to 3 per user; subjects 60 to 150 (roughly 6 to 8 per semester per branch); **subject offerings 400 to 1,500 — the largest academic table**; **students 2,000 to 6,000 — the dominant row count**; **marks 200,000 to 900,000 — the largest table by far**; examinations 5 to 30; class-teacher assignments 5 to 30; allocations 100 to 500; upload jobs, reports, audit logs and notifications all growing, with audit logs able to exceed 100,000 rows in a first term; refresh tokens 1 to 5 per active user; resets and invitations near zero. **Performance implication:** the marks table is the one that will stress the database, joined against students, offerings and academic years on every result computation. The compound unique on the mark idempotency key doubles as the index for the per-student result query, and the branch + semester index on students serves the class-roster and class-overview queries.

## 8. Technology stack

**Runtime and language.** Node.js 18 or newer (root engine constraint); pnpm 9 or newer, pinned to **9.12.3**; **Turborepo 2.3.3**; **TypeScript 5.6.3** at the root and on the frontend, with a compatible range on the backend and workers. Both TypeScript configurations set **strict**; the backend additionally enables strict null checks, no implicit any, strict bind/call/apply, no fallthrough in switch and consistent casing in file names, with strict property initialisation explicitly disabled (required by the generated database models). Target ES2022 throughout.

**Monorepo.** pnpm workspaces over application and package directory globs. Six post-install build scripts are whitelisted: the Nest core, the Prisma client and engines, bcrypt, msgpackr-extract and prisma. Build ordering is encoded as shared packages building before the apps.

**Backend framework.** **NestJS 10** on the Express platform (core, common and platform-express at 10.3), CLI and schematics at 10.3 and 10.1. Swagger at 7.3, generated at runtime in non-production only, so the document cannot drift from the code. The platform's WebSocket packages are declared dependencies but imported nowhere — notifications are HTTP-only. Architecture is controller to service to repository throughout; each module directory carries its own trio of files. Throttler at 5.1.

**Frontend framework.** **Next.js 14.2.18** with the App Router, **React 18.3.1** and React DOM 18.3.1, React strict mode on. The application directory is split into an authentication group and an authenticated group plus a root layout, a providers file and a global stylesheet. A middleware file exists but performs no route protection — by explicit design decision documented in the code, the session lives in local storage, which the edge runtime cannot read, so protection is enforced client-side in the authenticated layout.

**Database and ORM.** **Prisma 5.8** with the client generator, against **PostgreSQL on Neon Cloud** (serverless Postgres). Seven migration folders. The schema is driven by a single database URL. Prisma's project is deployed rather than developed, deliberately, because deploy never creates a migration file and is safe to re-run.

**Queue system.** **BullMQ 5.7** with the Nest integration at 10.1. Four queues: upload jobs, OCR jobs, report jobs, email jobs. **All four processors live in the backend**, not in the worker process. No processor declares an explicit concurrency, so every worker runs at BullMQ's default of one concurrent job. Retention keeps the last 50 on completion for report and email jobs and the last 100 on failure across the board; OCR and upload jobs are removed on completion. Email jobs use 3 attempts with exponential backoff from a 5-second delay.

**Cache.** **Redis via ioredis 5.3.** The client connects lazily, disables the offline queue, allows 2 retries per request, and gives up after 5 attempts with a 200ms-times-attempt backoff capped at 2000ms. The cache is explicitly an accelerator, never a source of truth: if Redis is unreachable every read returns null and every write is dropped, so the API keeps serving correct results straight from the database.

**Authentication.** **Passport-JWT** (Passport JWT 4.0, Nest Passport 10.0, Nest JWT module 10.2, Passport 0.7) — **not Better Auth, despite the README claim**. Password hashing is bcrypt 5.1.

**Validation.** **class-validator 0.14** and class-transformer 0.5 on the backend, applied through a global pipe with whitelisting, forbidding of non-whitelisted properties, transformation and implicit type conversion. On the frontend, **Zod 3.23.8** with the React Hook Form resolver at 3.9.1 and React Hook Form 7.53.2.

**Charting.** **Recharts 2.13.3.**

**UI component library.** shadcn/ui-style components on **Radix UI** primitives (avatar, collapsible, dialog, dropdown menu, label, progress, scroll area, select, separator, slot, switch, tabs, tooltip, visually-hidden), with **lucide-react 0.460.0** for icons. Styling is **Tailwind CSS 3.4.14** with tailwindcss-animate, class-variance-authority, clsx and tailwind-merge. The design tokens are the Coinbase palette, hand-transcribed into the Tailwind configuration. The component configuration uses the "new-york" shadcn style with server components enabled and lucide icons. A local component set exists for avatar, button, card, dropdown menu, input, search input, select, separator, sheet, sidebar, skeleton, tooltip and user avatar.

**Report generation.** **ExcelJS 4.4.0.** **There is no PDF-generation library** — no PDFKit, PDFMake, Puppeteer, Playwright or headless Chrome. **Reports are Excel only.**

**PDF reading.** `pdf-parse` 1.1.1 and `pdfjs-dist` 3.11.174 read PDFs; **`@napi-rs/canvas` 1.1.0** is the rasteriser that renders pages to images for the AI reader.

**Email.** **nodemailer 10.2** over generic SMTP, asynchronous through the email queue, templates as HTML files.

**Data fetching.** **TanStack React Query 5.59.20** with the devtools package at the same version. **Axios 1.7.7** with a 30-second timeout, credentials included, and interceptors that attach the bearer token and handle silent token refresh in a single flight.

**AI and OCR — two distinct providers.** **Hugging Face Inference API (2.6.4)** serves OCR of scanned marks statements: three models configured — a table transformer for structure recognition (max input 50 MB, table detection enabled), a handwriting recogniser (max input 10 MB), and a general-purpose OCR model (max input 10 MB) — selected by content hint, with table detection as a second optional call. Authentication is by an API token. **GROQ** (OpenAI-compatible chat completions) serves scheme reading: a hand-written client, not an SDK. The default reader model is a Qwen 27B instruct variant; the **guard model is Llama Prompt Guard 2 86M**, a prompt-injection guard that inspects a document *before* the reader sees it. **Object storage.** The AWS S3 client (3.1161) against any S3-compatible endpoint — Neon Storage in the intended deployment, but AWS S3, Cloudflare R2 and MinIO all work by changing the endpoint. A path-style flag exists because Neon Storage and MinIO put the bucket in the path while AWS and R2 put it in the hostname. With no bucket configured the service falls back to local-disk storage under a configurable directory.

**Logging.** Winston 3.11 with daily file rotation.

**Testing.** Jest 29.7 throughout, with ts-jest on the backend and workers and the Next.js Jest transform (SWC) on the frontend. The frontend needs a **custom jsdom environment** because the native canvas package is present but unbuilt on the machine and jsdom throws on startup if it tries to load it; a canvas mock is mapped in to stub the optional dependency, and the config must export an async function because the Next.js Jest transform is async. Testing Library on the frontend; supertest installed on the backend.

**Linting and formatting.** ESLint 8 at the root with the recommended and TypeScript-recommended sets, plus Prettier 3.2.4 with semicolons, single quotes, 2-space indent, ES5 trailing commas, a 100-character print width and always-parenthesised arrow parameters. ## 9. Security implementation

**Authentication.** Stateless bearer tokens. **Access 15 minutes; refresh 7 days**, signed with a **separate secret**. Refresh tokens are stored bcrypt-hashed and **rotated on every use** — the presented token is revoked and a replacement issued, so a stolen refresh token works at most once. Logout revokes one user's tokens; logout-all revokes every session.

**Password storage.** **bcrypt at 12 rounds** by default, configurable. Hashes are never returned by the API. Passwords are always hashed in the service layer; plaintext never reaches the data layer and no client-supplied hash is ever stored.

**Other token storage.** Invitation and password-reset tokens are stored as **SHA-256 hashes** only; the raw token exists solely in the emailed link. **Password-reset links expire after 30 minutes; invitation links after 168 hours (7 days).** Only the most recent reset link stays valid.

**Forced password change.** A global guard blocks every authenticated route while a change is pending. The client redirect is a convenience, not the control — without the guard, a token holder could skip the page and keep using the API with the old password. The flag is cached for 60 seconds.

**Authorisation.** Permission enforcement at the route layer, plus service-layer scope and ownership checks a decorator cannot express. **The permission cache falls back to the database on a miss and on a Redis outage** — returning "allow" on a miss would have silently disabled every permission check in the system.

**Rate limiting.** Three global tiers — **3 per second, 100 per minute, 1,000 per hour** — with per-route overrides in Section 10. A rate-limit response surfaces the server's retry window to the user in minutes.

**Input validation.** The global pipe strips unknown properties and **rejects requests carrying them**, with implicit type conversion. Data-transfer objects use class-validator with type, format and range constraints. An unexpected field becomes a 400 rather than a silently-ignored mass-assignment.

**Server-side request forgery protection.** Any URL the server is told to fetch is checked twice — at job creation and at the moment of fetching. The guard refuses non-HTTP(S) schemes, refuses known-local hostnames, and resolves the hostname, checking **every resolved address** against private, loopback, carrier-grade NAT, link-local (including the cloud metadata address), multicast and reserved ranges, for both IPv4 and IPv6. **The refusal message is identical for every refusal**, so the check cannot probe which addresses exist.

**File upload validation.** Type is decided from **magic bytes** rather than extension or declared content type, for both result files and avatars. Avatars are restricted to PNG, JPEG, GIF and WebP, capped at 5 MB, with the stored extension taken from the detected type so the stored file always matches its bytes. Result uploads cap at 50 MB; student imports at 5 MB; syllabus documents at 20 MB. **The file-download route requires authentication and an ownership check**, because a stored file may hold a result statement or a profile picture.

**Path traversal.** Every local storage path is resolved and checked against the storage root, so a crafted filename cannot escape it.

**Output encoding.** Email templates escape every interpolated value; URLs are escaped separately.

**CORS and headers.** CORS is enabled with a comma-separated origin allowlist and credentials enabled. **Helmet** is mounted globally as the first middleware, so every response carries the standard security headers. A cookie parser is installed and mounted.

**Error handling.** A single global exception filter normalises every error into a consistent envelope carrying a request identifier; internal detail never reaches the client.

**Enumeration resistance.** Password reset returns the same message whether or not the address exists. All invalid invitation tokens produce one indistinguishable message. Upload jobs belonging to another member return **not found**, not forbidden, so an identifier's existence cannot be probed. A student requesting another student's record receives forbidden — the record does exist — rather than not found.

**Prompt-injection defence.** A dedicated guard model inspects an externally-supplied document for instructions aimed at the reader **before** the reader sees it, and its verdict is recorded and surfaced to the reviewer. A failed guard check is reported as *not checked*, never as *checked and safe*.

**Two honest gaps.** **There is no CSRF token mechanism.** The application is token-authenticated via the Authorization header, so this is defensible today; but CORS has credentials enabled and a cookie parser is installed, so the intent should be confirmed — and it becomes a real gap the moment a cookie-based session is introduced. **There is no virus scanning on uploads**, despite the project rules mandating it; the current defence is type and magic-byte validation only.

**One more honesty point.** The two JWT secrets **fall back to a placeholder string when the environment variables are missing** — a startup failure would be safer than silently producing a known secret. The session tokens live in browser local storage rather than HTTP-only cookies, a weaker posture since any script on the page can reach them.

## 10. Performance and configuration facts

**Cache TTLs.** The backend's default is **300 seconds (5 minutes)**, applied to every cache write unless overridden. Finer-grained TTLs exist for specific keys: user permissions 5 minutes, branch list 1 hour, scheme list 1 hour, scheme rules 30 minutes, subjects list 1 hour. The forced-password-change flag is cached for 60 seconds and is deliberately not versioned by the permission counter, since password state has its own lifecycle. The permission cache is invalidated in constant time by a global version counter that any RBAC mutation bumps, orphaning every previously cached set at once rather than walking affected users.

**Frontend cache behaviour.** React Query **stale time 60 seconds**, garbage-collection 10 minutes, no refetch on window focus, refetch on reconnect, two retries with 4xx excluded, mutations never retried. Individual hooks override the stale time by volatility — reference data (branches, schemes, subjects, academic years, the permission catalogue) cached 5 to 30 minutes; lists and dashboard blocks 60 seconds; upload history 30 seconds. List queries keep the previous page on screen while the next loads. Invalidation is centralised in a module naming each affected key family explicitly rather than relying on prefix matching.

**Rate-limit tiers and route overrides.** Global: **3 per second, 100 per minute, 1,000 per hour.** Overrides: **login 5 per 15 minutes**; registration 10 per 15 minutes; forgot password 20 per 15 minutes; password reset 5 and 10 per 15 minutes; invitations 10 per 15 minutes; **upload 10 per hour**; **report generation 5 per hour**; scheme reader 10 per hour; analytics 30 per minute; dashboard 60 to 120 per minute.

**Upload size limits.** **50 MB**, expressed in four places that must agree — the environment-driven limit, the service constant, the multipart interceptor limit and the shared catalogue constant. The scheme reader has its own tighter **20 MB** limit for syllabus documents. Student imports cap at 5 MB and 5,000 rows.

**Pagination defaults.** Page 1, page size 20, maximum page size 100. The toppers query enforces a default limit of 10 with a floor of 1. The interface offers rows-per-page choices of 10, 20, 50 and 100.

**Timeouts.** The default HTTP request timeout is **30 seconds**, enforced by a global interceptor; individual routes may declare a longer one, which the scheme reader does because reading a syllabus PDF legitimately takes longer. The frontend HTTP client has a matching 30-second timeout. The GROQ client defaults to 60 seconds per attempt.

**AI pacing.** Calls to the GROQ reader are spaced a minimum of **31,000 ms apart**, with a 2,100 ms gap between guard calls, because the provider's per-minute output-token limit is the binding constraint. Output is capped at 450 tokens per call and input at 1,200 characters per call, so a large page is read in pieces rather than truncated. The cost estimate driving the page-splitting arithmetic is 40 tokens per course. The GROQ variables are read by the code but are **absent from the environment example file**, so an operator following that file alone will find the scheme reader degraded.

**Database connection behaviour.** Connection pooling is configured with a limit of **10 connections** and a pool timeout of 20 seconds, appended to the database URL if not already present, preserving existing secure-mode and binding parameters. A query extension retries the four transient connectivity error codes with exponential backoff from a 500 ms base, up to four attempts, because the serverless provider suspends after inactivity and the first request after a suspend pays the wake-up cost; one error code is deliberately excluded because the statement may already have executed. **Boot-time connection establishment is itself retried**, so the application starts rather than failing module initialisation against a cold provider.

**Queue concurrency and retention.** No processor sets an explicit concurrency, so all four run at BullMQ's default of **one concurrent job**.

**Polling intervals.** Uploads poll every **2 seconds** while pending or processing; reports every **3 seconds** while any report is queued or generating. Polling stops as soon as the work reaches a terminal state.

## 12. Deployment reality

**There is no deployment configuration in the repository.** An exhaustive search found **no Dockerfile, no container-compose file, no continuous-integration workflow directory, no platform configuration for any hosting provider, no process manager file, and no Kubernetes manifests or infrastructure-as-code of any kind.** The only YAML files are the workspace definition and the lock file. A backend script named as an asset copier is a build-time step, not a containerisation script.

**Intended hosting, inferred from the code and documentation.** The application is built for a **serverless** deployment:
- **Database** — Neon Cloud (serverless Postgres). The code is explicitly written around it: the pool is capped because the provider limits concurrent connections, transient connectivity errors are retried because the provider suspends after inactivity, and boot-time connection establishment is retried so the application starts against a cold provider.
- **Storage** — Neon Storage (S3-compatible), with AWS S3, Cloudflare R2 and MinIO supported by configuration, plus a local-disk fallback for fresh checkouts and continuous integration.
- **Cache and queue** — Redis, hosted or local, with the same database index serving both the cache and all four queues.
- **Frontend** — Next.js 14, deployable to any Node host or a serverless platform; the build and start entry points are conventional and no platform-specific settings are present.
- **Backend** — the Nest build produces a distribution directory and the production start runs the compiled entry point, with an asset-copy step after the build.

**Two deployment blockers worth flagging.** First, the backend's test and TypeScript configurations resolve the shared packages to their **built output**, so those packages must be built before the backend is built or started. The root orchestration encodes this correctly — builds depend on upstream package builds — so a root-level build handles it, but a bare backend-local build does not. Second, the worker process is currently a **supervisor**, so deploying it alongside the backend gains monitoring but **no additional job throughput** — the backend alone runs all four processors.

**Production configuration.** In production the API documentation is not mounted at all, and the CORS origin must name the real frontend origin. The two JWT secrets must be distinct real secrets; the configuration file's fallback value is a literal placeholder string, so a missing variable silently produces a known secret rather than a boot failure. The AI service credentials are optional — both the OCR and scheme-reader features degrade gracefully without them. The frontend needs the API base URL pointing at the deployed API.

## 14. Known implementation inconsistencies to describe honestly

**14.1 The pass-line inconsistency — the most significant.** The three scheme calculators use a **50% aggregate pass threshold**. The analytics module, the dashboard and the class overview use **40%**. A student at 45% is "Pass" in the calculator and "passing" in the analytics figures; the grade bands compound it, with the analytics layer treating 40% as a borderline band that still counts as a pass while the calculator would fail the same student. This is a divergence between the computation layer and the reporting layer and should be reconciled to one value. It is the most quotable defect in the system.

**14.2 Denominator mismatch within a single response.** Analytics averages are computed **per mark row** while pass/fail is computed **per student** — two figures in the same response use different denominators. The dashboard's trend line was corrected to aggregate per student first, because averaging rows would weight a student with more components more heavily; the analytics module retains the per-row averaging.

**14.3 No re-evaluation workflow.** The withheld flag and the audited correction route exist, but there is no re-evaluator role and no provisional-versus-final result states. A genuine re-evaluation process — a student applying, a re-evaluator appointed, an interim mark, a revised mark — has no representation.

**14.4 No attendance model.** Attendance does not participate in pass/fail and is not modelled as a mark component. Many colleges make attendance a minimum-eligibility condition; the system cannot express that.

**14.5 No CSRF protection while CORS allows credentials.** Authentication is bearer-token based, which makes CSRF largely inapplicable, but CORS has credentials enabled and a cookie parser is installed, so the intent should be confirmed and the gap becomes real the moment a cookie-based session is introduced. There is also **no virus scanning** of uploads, which the project rules mandate.

**14.6 JWT secrets fall back to a placeholder string if unset.** The configuration file's fallback is a literal placeholder, so a missing environment variable silently produces a known secret rather than a boot failure. The same honesty point applies to the local-storage session, a weaker posture than HTTP-only cookies.

**14.7 The middleware does not enforce authentication.** It exists only to let public paths through; the gate lives in the authenticated layout, because the session lives in local storage and the edge runtime cannot read it. The code documents this deliberately, but a protected route is guarded client-side, with the server as the real boundary. Report as a deliberate trade-off, not an oversight.

**14.8 Client-side and server-side filtering are mixed** across list pages without a uniform rule — the subjects page resolves its placement filters client-side through the offerings list because a subject row carries no branch under the split model.

**14.9 React Hook Form and Zod are installed but barely used.** The forms use hand-written controlled state, so the dormant client validator can drift out of step with the server's data-transfer objects, which are the real source of truth.

**14.10 Interface gaps.** No dark mode anywhere (the design system provides none, so none was invented); **no column-header sorting on any list** — every list is fixed-order; no bulk selection actions beyond the two file-driven flows; the audit log has **no detail view and no export**, despite the backend exposing a detail endpoint the page never calls; and the raw-JSON scheme rules editor contradicts the project's own rule against raw JSON in the interface.

**14.11 Interface conventions that are deliberate and should not be reported as defects.** The academic-years screen is read-only with no create button because the backend exposes no create endpoint; the middleware performs no route protection for the documented local-storage reason; there is no "remember me" checkbox because the session persists regardless; and the dashboard's publication-status query is guarded so a not-yet-migrated table degrades gracefully rather than showing a misleading zero.

## 16. A short note for the chapter writers

Write in plain English, business and technical language, for an academic readership. Prefer "the examination cell enters marks" over internal identifiers. Give real numbers — 45 permissions, 23 entities, 31 indexes, 22 role-permission bindings for the Super Administrator, 50 MB upload cap, 15-minute access tokens, 168-hour invitation lifetime — because a report full of vague adjectives reads as padding.

Where a claim is uncertain across the briefs, say "not established" rather than guessing. In particular: the count of API endpoints is not established (the API is URI-versioned under a single prefix and the surface was read per-module rather than enumerated); the number of frontend pages is established as the route inventory in Section 3 but should not be converted into a headline count; and the number of charts is six distinct chart types across two screens.

Do not describe anything as "secure" without qualification. Say what the mechanism is and what it does not cover — there is no CSRF token mechanism, no virus scanning, and the JWT secrets have a placeholder fallback. Do not claim soft deletes or an enforced coverage threshold; both are stated project goals the code does not implement. Do not claim a deployment exists; there are no Dockerfiles, no continuous-integration workflows and no platform configuration, and the hosting model is inferred from serverless-oriented code.

Where the report must choose between a stale document and the running system, choose the running system — and, where it improves the report, say that the document was stale and why it matters.
